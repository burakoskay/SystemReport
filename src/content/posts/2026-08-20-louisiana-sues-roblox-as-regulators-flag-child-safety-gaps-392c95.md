## Louisiana sues Roblox over predator exposure
Roblox faces a state lawsuit that accuses the platform of enabling sexual exploitation of minors. The case arrives on the heels of a regulator’s report that the company’s recent safety tweaks still let adults contact children without parental consent.

Attorney General Liz Murrill filed the complaint in Baton Rouge, naming Roblox’s 380 million monthly users – more than half of whom are aged 9 to 16 – as a vulnerable audience. She alleges that Roblox “created, curated and perpetuated an online environment where child sex predators thrive, unite, hunt and victimize kids.” The filing lists games such as “Escape to Epstein Island,” “Survive Diddy,” and “Public Bathroom Simulator Vibe” that allegedly depict simulated sexual activity, including child gang‑rape scenarios. Murrill also points to a July arrest in Livingston Parish where deputies seized a man using voice‑altering software to pose as a 14‑year‑old girl on Roblox.

## Regulator finds four critical safety failures
A separate regulatory review identified four major failings in Roblox’s child‑protection system. The most glaring gap is the ability for adults to send connection requests to minors without any parental approval step. The report also notes insufficient age verification, a lack of proactive warnings to parents, and inadequate monitoring of user‑generated content that can slip past automated filters.

Roblox had announced changes to its safety suite earlier this year, but the regulator’s findings suggest those updates are superficial. The agency did not cite any quantitative improvement in incident rates, leaving the platform’s risk profile essentially unchanged. The report’s language is blunt: the current safeguards are “inadequate” for a service that markets itself as kid‑friendly.

## Louisiana’s HB61 and the national push for stricter online age controls
While the lawsuit targets Roblox directly, the state is also moving to close the broader loophole that lets minors create accounts without parental consent. HB61, passed unanimously by the Louisiana legislature, would ban “interactive computer services” from allowing users under 18 to sign up for their own accounts unless a parent authorizes the registration. The bill’s definition of online services is expansive enough to cover social media platforms, email providers, and popular games such as Roblox and Fortnite.

If Governor John Bel Edwards signs the measure, it would take effect in August 2024. The legislation also grants parents the power to cancel any existing terms‑of‑service contracts their children entered into. Critics, including NetChoice vice president Carl Szabo, warn that the bill infringes on First Amendment rights and would force massive data collection on Louisiana residents. The state is not alone: Utah, Arkansas, and several other states have passed or are debating comparable age‑verification mandates, and a federal Protecting Kids on Social Media Act is moving through Congress.

## Industry response and the road ahead
Roblox has not publicly commented on the Louisiana lawsuit or the regulator’s findings. The company’s typical response to safety concerns has been to point to its moderation tools and parental‑control settings, but neither the lawsuit nor the report references any recent upgrades that address the four identified failings.

Lawmakers in other states are watching Louisiana’s actions closely. If the lawsuit leads to a settlement that forces Roblox to overhaul its age‑verification and messaging systems, it could set a de‑facto standard for all child‑focused platforms. Conversely, a court dismissal could embolden other companies to maintain the status quo while lobbying against HB61.

## What to watch
Track the governor’s decision on HB61 and any court filings that follow the Louisiana lawsuit. A settlement that mandates real‑time age verification or bans certain user‑generated games would reshape how Roblox and similar services operate. Equally important is the regulator’s next step: whether it will impose fines, require a compliance timeline, or refer the matter to a federal agency. The next six months will reveal whether the industry can adapt or whether a wave of state‑level bans will force a more radical redesign of online play for kids.
