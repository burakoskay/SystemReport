Google Messages for Android is being tested with a new homepage that pins chat avatars, drops the search bar to the bottom, and tucks a Gemini shortcut into the corner. The three core tweaks aim to make the most‑used parts of the app reachable without a tap.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5][^6][^7][^8]

The change was reported by 9to5Google, which noted the redesign also ships with a fresh app icon.[^1][^2][^3][^4] No rollout date was given; the experiment is limited to a subset of users who have opted into the test program.[^1][^2]

## A hands‑on look at the new layout

Opening the app under the test shows a grid of recent conversations, each topped by a circular avatar that stays visible as you scroll.[^1][^3][^4][^6][^7] In the current stable version, avatars appear only when you tap a thread, forcing a quick glance up and down the list. The pinned avatars keep the visual cue in place, which feels like a small but noticeable speed‑up for anyone juggling dozens of chats.[^3]

Below the conversation grid sits a search field that used to sit at the top of the screen. Moving it to the bottom mirrors the placement of the new Gemini button, a small AI‑assistant icon that sits in the lower‑right corner.[^1][^3][^4][^5][^6][^7][^8] The bottom‑aligned search feels more thumb‑friendly on larger phones, and the Gemini shortcut invites users to experiment with AI‑driven replies without leaving the conversation view.

## Daily friction drops and new habits

For a power user who flips between work Slack, personal iMessage, and group chats, the new layout reduces the number of swipes needed to find a contact. The pinned avatars act as a visual index; you can skim the grid and spot the right face without opening each thread.[^3] That alone cuts a few seconds per day, which adds up over weeks.

The bottom search also changes the muscle memory built around the old top‑bar layout. Users who habitually tap the top edge will need to adjust, but the change aligns Messages with other Android apps that favor bottom navigation for larger screens.[^1][^3][^4][^5][^6][^8] The Gemini button introduces a new interaction pattern: tapping it opens a lightweight AI pane where you can draft replies, ask for quick translations, or generate short summaries.[^3] Early testers report that the AI suggestions feel generic at first, but they are useful for routine replies like confirming a meeting time.

## How the redesign fits into Messages' evolution

Google launched Messages in 2014 as a replacement for the stock SMS app, promising richer RCS features. Over the years the UI has seen incremental tweaks—new bubble styles, dark mode, and a redesign of the conversation view in 2020. The current experiment is the most visible overhaul since that 2020 refresh.

Historically, Google has used A/B testing to roll out UI changes. The company often ships a new icon or color palette to a small user base before committing to a global update. This pattern suggests that the pinned avatars and bottom search are still experimental; the company will likely collect usage metrics before deciding whether to replace the existing homepage.[^1][^2][^4]

## Competition and broader UI trends

Apple’s iMessage has long kept the conversation list at the top with a persistent search bar.[^1][^2][^4][^6][^7] Samsung’s One UI, slated for a 2027 beta, is also experimenting with bottom‑heavy navigation. The shift toward bottom‑aligned controls reflects a broader trend in mobile design, where larger phones make top‑edge taps harder for the thumb.[^1][^3][^5][^8]

Other messaging apps—WhatsApp, Telegram, Signal—have begun adding AI shortcuts or quick‑reply widgets. Google’s Gemini integration is a direct response to that pressure, offering a native AI assistant without requiring a third‑party plugin.[^3] By placing Gemini in the corner of the homepage, Google signals that AI will be a first‑class feature rather than an afterthought.[^3]

## What to watch next

The test is still limited, so the next data point will be the size of the rollout.[^1][^2] If Google expands the experiment to a broader user base, the company will likely publish a blog post or a release note detailing the rationale. Watch for any changes to the Gemini button’s functionality—whether it evolves from a simple suggestion engine to a deeper conversational partner. Finally, keep an eye on Android’s upcoming One UI 9.5 beta; a similar bottom‑focused layout could hint at industry‑wide convergence on thumb‑friendly design.
