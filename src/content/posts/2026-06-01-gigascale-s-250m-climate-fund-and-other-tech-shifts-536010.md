Mike Schroepfer’s Gigascale Capital closed a $250 million climate fund, signaling a surge of deep‑tech capital into energy‑scarcity solutions. The fund targets founders tackling the twin crises of power shortages and raw‑material constraints.

Schroepfer, the former Meta CTO, announced the raise in a brief blog post that listed early backers from the venture‑capital world. Gigascale’s mandate is to back hardware‑intensive startups that can scale climate‑positive impact without relying on incremental software tweaks. The timing aligns with a wave of corporate pledges to reach net‑zero, yet many of those pledges lack concrete hardware roadmaps.

## Gigascale Capital’s Climate Play

The $250 million pool dwarfs most recent climate‑tech raises, which typically hover around $50 million to $100 million. By concentrating on “energy‑intensive” problems, Gigascale hopes to fill a financing gap that traditional VC firms avoid because of long development cycles and high capital expenditure.

Schroepfer’s own background gives the fund credibility. He oversaw Meta’s data‑center expansion and helped the company achieve a 30 percent reduction in PUE (Power Usage Effectiveness) over five years. Those credentials suggest he can spot hardware breakthroughs that actually move the needle on emissions.

Critics note that throwing money at hardware does not guarantee climate outcomes. Without clear policy support, many prototypes stall at the lab stage. The fund’s success will hinge on whether its portfolio companies can secure downstream contracts from utilities or manufacturers before the next regulatory cycle.

## Revolut’s India Test Run

Revolut has begun serving a handful of Indian users ahead of a nationwide launch, leveraging a waitlist that now exceeds 450,000 prospects.[^6][^7][^8] The fintech’s early‑access rollout offers local bank transfers, crypto swaps, and a revamped UI tuned for low‑bandwidth connections.

The company’s Indian strategy mirrors its expansion playbook in the UK and US: build hype with a massive sign‑up funnel, then open the product to a controlled cohort. Early adopters report that the app feels “snappier” than local rivals, but they also flag higher fees on cross‑border remittances.

India’s fintech market is crowded, with homegrown players like Paytm and PhonePe holding dominant market share.[^6] Revolut’s entry raises the stakes for global challengers that must navigate RBI regulations, data‑localization rules, and a consumer base that still trusts legacy banks.

## ROG’s Xbox Ally X20 and AR Glass Bundle

ASUS’s Republic of Gamers brand unveiled the Xbox Ally X20, a handheld console that swaps the previous LCD for a crisp OLED screen and adds refined thumbsticks, haptic triggers, and a larger battery. The hardware refresh promises deeper blacks and smoother motion in handheld gaming sessions.

However, the X20 is not sold standalone. Buyers must purchase a bundled package that includes a pair of AR glasses priced well above the console itself. The glasses aim to overlay game telemetry and navigation cues, but early reviewers call the price “prohibitive” for the average gamer.

The bundling strategy reflects a broader industry trend: manufacturers use premium accessories to boost average selling price. For ROG, the gamble is whether the added AR experience justifies the cost or alienates the core handheld audience that values portability above all else.

## Security Alerts: Malicious npm Packages in Red Hat Cloud

Red Hat’s Cloud Services team disclosed a wave of malicious npm packages that slipped into its managed environments last quarter. The packages, masquerading as legitimate JavaScript libraries, executed supply‑chain attacks that could exfiltrate credentials from customer workloads.

Red Hat responded by revoking the compromised packages, issuing patches, and publishing a detailed incident report on its GitHub issue tracker. The firm urged customers to audit their dependency trees and enable automated vulnerability scanning.

Supply‑chain threats have risen sharply across the open‑source ecosystem, and the Red Hat episode underscores the need for tighter provenance checks. Enterprises that rely on managed cloud services must now treat third‑party libraries with the same scrutiny they apply to container images.

## Open‑Source Shifts: Nvidia Cosmos 3, Honor’s Canada Push, and the Kefir Compiler End

Nvidia announced Cosmos 3, a platform designed for physical AI reasoning that lets developers train models on real‑world interaction data. The release builds on earlier Cosmos versions, adding support for higher‑resolution sensor streams and tighter integration with Nvidia’s GPU stack.

At the same time, Chinese smartphone maker Honor is probing a market entry into Canada, according to a recent report. The company is reportedly negotiating with local carriers and regulators to determine whether it can meet Canadian compliance standards.

In a quieter but notable move, the maintainers of the Kefir C compiler declared the cessation of public development. The announcement, posted on the project’s website, cited dwindling contributor activity and a strategic shift toward newer compiler technologies.

Each of these developments reflects a broader theme: the open‑source and hardware worlds are converging, but the pace of innovation is increasingly gated by security concerns, regulatory hurdles, and the economics of sustaining long‑term projects.

## What to Watch

Track Gigascale Capital’s first deployment rounds; the performance of its portfolio will signal whether deep‑tech climate funding can move beyond hype. Keep an eye on Revolut’s regulatory filings with the RBI as the fintech scales across India. Monitor consumer reaction to the Xbox Ally X20‑AR bundle to see if premium accessories become a standard expectation. Finally, watch Red Hat’s next security bulletin for any follow‑up on supply‑chain hardening, and watch Nvidia’s developer roadmap for Cosmos 3 extensions.
