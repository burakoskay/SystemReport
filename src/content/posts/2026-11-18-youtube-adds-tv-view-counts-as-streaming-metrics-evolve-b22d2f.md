YouTube will start adding a public view count when users watch videos on a TV. The change arrives as the platform tweaks how it reports engagement across devices.

Engadget reported that the new metric will appear alongside the existing counter that tracks browser and mobile views. Viewers will see a combined total that reflects both traditional screens and living‑room sets. The rollout will begin next month and will apply to all public videos that are not hidden or age‑restricted.

## Why view counts matter now

Creators rely on view numbers to gauge audience interest and to negotiate brand deals. Advertisers use the same data to price inventory and to measure campaign reach. A higher count can push a video into YouTube’s recommendation engine, which in turn drives more ad impressions. The TV metric could shift the balance toward content that performs well on larger screens.

For channels that specialize in cooking, fitness, or long‑form documentaries, the TV boost may translate into a noticeable bump in reported views. Smaller creators who focus on mobile‑first short clips may see little change. The split will force creators to think about how they format videos for the couch versus the pocket.

## The broader shift to more efficient streaming

The timing coincides with the rollout of AV1, a video codec championed by Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Netflix, and other heavyweights. Gabe Frost, director of the Alliance for Open Media, said AV1 matches HEVC and VP9 quality while using 30 % to 40 % less network capacity. The reduction lowers bandwidth costs for both providers and viewers.

AV1 support is already landing in browsers. Mozilla’s Firefox includes a test build, and major browsers plan full rollout in 2018 and 2019. Hardware decoding will follow in 2020 as Intel, Nvidia, AMD, and Arm integrate the codec into new chips. The combined effect of better compression and more visible view counts could reshape how streaming services allocate resources.

## Rights owners push back on YouTube’s metrics

Former U2 manager Paul McGuinness has long criticized YouTube for underpaying performers, writers, and publishers. In a recent interview he argued that the internet does not share revenue fairly with rights holders. He said the debate has moved from illegal downloading to a demand for transparency.

McGuinness’ remarks highlight a tension between platform metrics and creator compensation. When YouTube inflates public view numbers, it may create the illusion of higher engagement without guaranteeing additional revenue. Rights owners worry that the metric could be used to justify lower royalty rates.

## Power users turn to terminal tools

While most viewers interact with YouTube through a graphical UI, a niche community prefers command‑line control. The mpvc‑tui project offers an mpc‑like interface for the mpv media player. It runs on Linux, macOS, and BSD and leverages POSIX utilities such as socat and awk.

The tool integrates with fzf and other terminal UI components, allowing users to script playlist management and stream selection. For engineers who monitor view counts on a TV while tweaking playback pipelines, mpvc‑tui provides a lightweight alternative to heavyweight desktop apps.

## What to watch next

YouTube will publish the exact algorithm that merges TV and non‑TV views in its next developer blog. The industry will watch for any changes to ad‑revenue calculations that accompany the metric. At the same time, AV1 hardware support will land in consumer devices throughout 2020, promising smoother playback on the very TVs that now display view counts. Finally, rights groups are expected to file new transparency requests with the European Commission later this year. Tracking those filings will reveal whether higher view numbers translate into better payouts for creators.
