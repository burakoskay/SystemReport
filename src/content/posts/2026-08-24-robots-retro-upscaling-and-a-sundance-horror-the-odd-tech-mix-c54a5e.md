## A Sundance horror that feels like a glitch in reality

The newest buzz around indie cinema is less about glossy trailers and more about the uneasy feeling of watching a teenager stare into a camera while a cursed challenge unfolds. Jane Schoenbrun’s "We’re All Going to the World’s Fair" delivers that vibe in a 2021 Sundance debut that still feels fresh in 2024. The film follows Casey, a high‑schooler who tackles the so‑called World’s Fair Challenge—a hybrid of an alternate‑reality game and an internet dare that mixes a creepy mantra, a splash of blood, and a mysterious video clip. The entire spectacle happens in Casey’s bedroom, and the camera never leaves her gaze.

The challenge is essentially an ARG creepypasta turned into a personal performance art piece. The audience watches a single teenager execute a speedrun of horror tropes while the world outside remains unseen. The result is an intimate, almost claustrophobic study of digital rites of passage. The Verge notes that the film’s premise feels like a “speedrun of horror movie tropes” delivered by a lone girl in her room. That narrow focus forces viewers to confront how online challenges can become self‑contained rituals, a point that feels especially relevant as platforms continue to amplify viral dares.

## A $449 laptop that feels like a funhouse mirror

If you ever wanted a machine that reminds you of a MacBook but refuses to behave like one, Chuwi’s Unibook might be the closest thing. Priced at $449, the Unibook lands in the entry‑level market with a design that looks like a distorted MacBook Neo. Ars Technica calls it a “weird entry‑level laptop for people with patience,” and the description fits: the chassis is sleek, the keyboard feels cheap, and the performance hovers just above the threshold of usability.

The device is not a workhorse; it is a test of endurance. The review points out that the Unibook will run basic productivity tools without complaint, but anything more demanding quickly reveals its limits. The price tag makes it tempting for students or hobbyists who need a portable screen, yet the trade‑off is a sluggish SSD and a display that feels more like a novelty than a professional panel. For a market saturated with high‑end ultrabooks, the Unibook’s existence underscores how manufacturers still chase niche corners—people who want a recognizable form factor without the premium price.

## The price of going big: why IMAX stays out of reach for most

Shooting an IMAX movie still reads like a financial gamble. Engadget explains that the format requires specialized cameras, larger film stock, and a crew that can handle the bulk of the equipment. Those factors drive budgets up dramatically, which explains why most filmmakers shy away from the format for now. The cost barrier keeps IMAX releases limited to blockbuster franchises that can justify the expense.

The same economic logic applies to high‑fidelity audio. Dolby Digital 5.1 and Dolby Digital Plus both promise immersive sound, but the latter adds higher bitrate and more channels, demanding newer hardware for full playback. Engadget notes that equipment support varies, meaning studios must decide whether the marginal gain in audio quality is worth the additional cost for the average viewer. The decision mirrors the IMAX dilemma: premium experiences stay premium because the infrastructure to deliver them isn’t universal.

## When robots outrun humans and old games get a 4K facelift

Humanoid robots recently posted a 100‑meter dash time that beats Usain Bolt’s world record. The achievement is as absurd as it is impressive, with the machines looking “absolutely ridiculous” as they sprint. The headline‑grabbing result says more about robotics research than about athletics; it shows that engineers can now coordinate balance, torque, and speed in a package that still looks like a clunky prototype.

At the same time, retro gaming is getting its own high‑tech makeover. The Analogue 3D device upscales Nintendo 64 titles to 4K resolution, delivering sharper textures and smoother edges while preserving the original feel. Engadget praises the upscaler’s ability to enhance old games but warns of “a few caveats,” such as occasional artifacting on complex scenes. The device proves that even decades‑old software can benefit from modern display technology, provided the hardware respects the source material’s limitations.

## What to watch next

The convergence of low‑budget hardware, high‑cost cinematic formats, and breakthrough robotics raises a simple question: which niche will break into the mainstream next? Keep an eye on Chuwi’s upcoming revisions of the Unibook; a modest performance bump could push the device into broader adoption among students. Watch the next wave of IMAX‑compatible streaming services—if they can lower the barrier for high‑resolution video, the format might finally leave the theater floor. Finally, track the next public sprint by humanoid robots; if they can shave milliseconds off the current record, the conversation will shift from novelty to genuine competition. The next data point in any of these arenas will signal whether the oddities we see now are fleeting experiments or the seeds of a new standard.
