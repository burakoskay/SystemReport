## China stalls Chang’e 7 until 2027
China announced that the Chang’e 7 mission will not fly this year. The agency said the spacecraft does not meet the conditions for launch. The delay pushes the launch to 2027, a timeline the program calls "absolute success".

The postponement hurts a tightly packed lunar calendar. Chang’e 7 was slated to map the Moon’s far side, test a lander, and scout resources for future bases. Missing the window means other nations will race ahead on similar objectives. The Chinese space bureau has not released a new flight‑date beyond the year marker.

## Radiation shielding gets a fungal boost
One of the technical hurdles for any lunar or Martian venture is radiation. A study on the International Space Station examined Cladosporium sphaerospermum, a dematiaceous radiotrophic fungus that thrives near Chernobyl. Over a 30‑day experiment, a 1.7 mm thick fungal lawn cut ionizing radiation by 2.17 % ± 0.25 compared with a control. The same fungus grew 21 % faster in microgravity, suggesting it can adapt to space‑borne radiation.

If the results scale, a self‑replicating shield could be grown on a lunar habitat rather than shipped from Earth. The passive protection would complement existing aluminum and polyethylene layers. The authors disclosed no competing interests, keeping the data clean but still early‑stage.

## Lessons from Earth’s fiery past
Volcanoes have reshaped climate and societies on a global scale. Enormous eruptions have injected ash and sulfur into the stratosphere, cooling temperatures for years. Those historic events remind us that planetary bodies can undergo abrupt, environment‑altering changes.

For lunar planners, the lesson is clear: any surface operation must anticipate sudden hazards, whether from micrometeorite impacts or unexpected dust storms. The Chinese delay may be a pre‑emptive move to avoid a repeat of past missteps that left crews vulnerable.

## Asimov’s foresight meets modern ambition
Isaac Asimov predicted that by 2014 only unmanned ships would have landed on Mars, while a manned expedition would be in the works. He also imagined laser‑based communication handling “any number of simultaneous conversations between earth and moon.” Those ideas echo today’s push for high‑bandwidth laser links between lunar orbiters and Earth stations.

Asimov’s broader vision of robotics and autonomous vehicles also aligns with Chang’e 7’s rover and lander suite. The author warned that robots would be “neither common nor very good” in 2014, yet he saw them existing. Decades later, China is fielding sophisticated rovers that can navigate the Moon’s far side with minimal human oversight.

## A game‑dev community mirrors real‑world stakes
While engineers wrestle with fungal shields and launch windows, a small open‑source team is building Naev, a space‑exploration game. Naev lets players trade, fight, and complete missions across a procedural galaxy. The developers openly ask for story ideas, art, sound, and code contributions.

The game’s community‑driven model reflects the collaborative spirit needed for lunar endeavors. Just as Naev relies on volunteers to mature, large‑scale space projects increasingly lean on public‑sector partnerships and citizen science to fill capability gaps.
