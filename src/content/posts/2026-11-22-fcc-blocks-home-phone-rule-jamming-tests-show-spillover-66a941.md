## FCC preempts California's home‑phone mandate
The Federal Communications Commission said California cannot force AT&T to keep a legacy home‑phone line alive. The agency invoked its preemption authority to nullify a state rule that required the carrier to maintain the service beyond the end of the copper network. The decision appeared in an FCC filing that cited federal jurisdiction over interstate communications. 

AT&T will no longer be bound by the California Public Utilities Commission's order that dated back to the era of dial‑tone landlines. The FCC ruling means the company can discontinue the service for residential customers who have not explicitly asked to keep it. The move follows a broader trend of regulators pushing back against state‑level attempts to preserve outdated telephony infrastructure.

## CTIA jamming tests reveal spillover beyond prison walls
The wireless trade group CTIA reported that a recent test of cellular‑jamming equipment in a correctional facility produced interference up to two and a half miles away. The test was meant to block contraband phones inside the prison, but the signal leaked into neighboring neighborhoods. 

CTIA’s press release noted that the unintended reach could affect nearby lawful users, potentially disrupting emergency calls and everyday data traffic. The organization said the findings underscore the need for tighter controls on jamming deployments, especially as more jurisdictions consider the technology to curb illicit phone use in detention centers.

## Consumer fallout from regulatory tug‑of‑war
When the FCC strips a state rule, residential customers lose a safety net. Many households still rely on a landline for backup power during outages, for hearing‑aid compatibility, or for simple voice‑only communication. The California rule was one of the few that explicitly protected those use cases. 

Without the mandate, AT&T can prioritize fiber, 5G, and other modern services. The company may still offer a landline on a voluntary basis, but pricing and availability will be at its discretion. For consumers, the loss of a guaranteed fallback could translate into higher risk during storms or blackouts.

## The technical trade‑off of jamming in a crowded spectrum
Cellular jammers emit a broad‑band signal that drowns out legitimate traffic. In a prison setting, the goal is to silence smuggled smartphones that facilitate contraband trade and coordination. However, the CTIA test showed that the jammer's field did not respect the prison perimeter. 

The spillover distance of roughly 2.5 miles means the jammer can interfere with carriers’ towers that serve thousands of users. That interference can degrade voice quality, drop data sessions, and even block 911 calls if the jammer’s frequency overlaps emergency bands. Engineers must balance the need for security inside facilities with the potential for collateral disruption.

## Broader phone‑use trends: Android pitfalls and iOS refinements
While regulators wrestle over legacy landlines and jamming, everyday phone users face a different set of challenges. Engadget recently compiled a list of actions Android owners should avoid to preserve device longevity. The guide warns against installing apps from unknown sources, overcharging batteries, and disabling security updates. Those missteps can shorten a phone’s useful life and expose users to malware.

On the iOS side, 9to5Mac highlighted non‑AI enhancements in the iOS 27 release, such as a personalized Smart Reply in Messages that learns a user’s writing style. The feature, while modest, demonstrates Apple’s focus on incremental usability improvements rather than headline‑grabbing AI hype. Together, these updates illustrate a market where both Android and iOS vendors are fine‑tuning the everyday experience, even as regulators debate the relevance of legacy services.

## What to watch
The FCC’s preemption may prompt other states to revisit their own home‑phone protections. Watch for filings in New York, Texas, and the Midwest as utilities test the limits of federal authority. At the same time, CTIA’s spillover data will likely fuel congressional hearings on the permissible scope of jamming technology. Lawmakers may demand stricter licensing or geographic constraints before approving future deployments. The next few months will reveal whether the balance tips toward modern broadband dominance or a renewed emphasis on protecting legacy voice services.
