## Premium price bump hits YouTube users
YouTube Premium users see their subscription cost rise this month. The increase follows a prior announcement that the service would become more expensive.
The hike arrives without a disclosed percentage or new price point. Existing subscribers must decide whether the ad‑free experience still justifies the higher bill. Some users may drop the service, especially if they already tolerate occasional ads on the free tier.
The move signals that even entrenched platforms feel pressure to extract more revenue. When a service that already commands a premium price nudges its fees upward, it tests the loyalty of a user base that knows how to switch to alternatives.

## A cheap hack restores Android Auto in GM EVs
General Motors continues to refuse phone‑based Android Auto and CarPlay in its electric models. Chevrolet and Cadillac owners cannot stream phone apps to the dashboard as they could in gasoline‑powered siblings.
A third‑party adapter now bridges that gap. The device plugs into the car’s infotainment port and re‑enables the missing smartphone integration. It works for now, but it depends on a workaround rather than an official software fix.
The adapter highlights a tension between manufacturers’ platform choices and consumer expectations. Drivers who bought a GM EV for its tech promise now face a DIY solution to regain a feature they assumed was standard.

## Canada’s bill C‑10 amendment expands broadcast rules to online video
The House of Commons Heritage committee cleared an amendment that lets the federal government regulate video posts on YouTube and other social platforms the same way it regulates national broadcasting. The change removes the exemption that previously shielded user‑generated content.
Critics argue the move attacks free expression. Former CRTC commissioner Peter Menzies warned that granting a government agency authority over legal user content amounts to a “full‑blown assault” on democratic foundations. Heritage Minister Steven Guilbeault’s office says the amendment targets professional series, films, and music, not everyday Canadians posting videos.
Professor Michael Geist of the University of Ottawa echoed the concern, noting that a user’s TikTok clip or YouTube upload would be treated as a program subject to the CRTC. The amendment also aims to tighten control over music streaming on social media, a goal the government says requires new safeguards.

## Nepal blocks major social platforms over registration dispute
Nepal’s government announced a block on most social media apps, including Facebook, X, and YouTube. The block took effect after the companies failed to register with the state as required by a pending bill.
Minister for Communication and Information Prithvi Subba Gurung said about two dozen platforms received repeated notices to register. Those that complied—TikTok, Viber, and three others—remain online. The legislation seeks to ensure platforms are “properly managed, responsible and accountable.”
Rights groups denounce the bill as a censorship tool. They argue it punishes opponents who voice protests online and violates fundamental freedoms. Officials claim the law is needed to monitor content and hold operators accountable for what users share.

## What to watch next
In the United States, YouTube will likely monitor churn rates after the Premium hike and may adjust pricing or features to retain subscribers. In the automotive space, GM’s stance on smartphone integration could prompt other OEMs to either double down on proprietary systems or adopt third‑party adapters at scale. In Canada, the CRTC is expected to draft the detailed rules that will enforce the Bill C‑10 amendment; watch for the first set of compliance notices to streaming services. In Nepal, the pending registration bill will move through parliament; its final language will determine whether the current block expands or eases. Tracking these developments will reveal how platform owners balance revenue, user experience, and regulatory compliance.
