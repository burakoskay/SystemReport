## Night vision from orbit
Anduril’s first satellite can track moving targets while the Earth is in total darkness. The company says the payload’s dynamic range lets it see objects that would be invisible to conventional sensors.
The hardware flies on a low‑Earth‑orbit platform launched earlier this year. In a brief demo, the sensor captured a maneuvering test vehicle against a star‑filled sky, proving the claim of “full dark” capability. Anduril’s defense customers will likely use the data for tracking hostile aircraft or missiles that hide behind the night side of the planet.
What sets the system apart is not a new antenna shape but a sensor that can swing between bright sunlight and pitch‑black without saturating. That flexibility reduces the need for multiple satellites tuned to different lighting conditions. For a company that already sells autonomous border‑monitoring kits, the satellite adds a strategic layer that extends its reach beyond ground‑based radar.
The move also signals Anduril’s ambition to compete with traditional aerospace firms that have long dominated space‑based ISR. If the dynamic‑range claim holds up in real combat, the satellite could force a rethink of how the military fields night‑time surveillance.

## SpaceX throttles Starlink for Ukraine
SpaceX has actively blocked Ukrainian forces from using Starlink on offensive drones.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5][^6][^7][^8] Gwynne Shotwell told reporters that the company “has done things to limit their ability to do that.”[^1][^3][^4]
Shotwell said Starlink was never intended to be weaponized.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^7] The statement came after months of reports that Ukrainian units relied on the service for both communications and targeting. In October, the signal was reportedly restricted past the front line, hampering Ukrainian advances.[^2] A source told CNN the outage “affected every effort of the Ukrainians to push past that front.”
SpaceX’s stance is consistent with Elon Musk’s public discomfort about the technology’s role in the war. Musk previously angered Kyiv by proposing a peace plan that ceded disputed territories. The company’s refusal to sell the service to the Pentagon for military use further underscores the tension between commercial intent and battlefield reality.
The decision has real consequences on the ground. Ukrainian troops still praise Starlink for keeping units connected and improving artillery accuracy, but the selective shutdowns force commanders to fall back on slower, less reliable radio links. The episode illustrates how a private satellite network can become a lever of geopolitical influence.

## Apple’s secret push for direct‑to‑phone satellite links
Apple is assembling a team of aerospace engineers to explore satellite communication that talks straight to iPhones. Bloomberg reports the hires include former Skybox Imaging executives Michael Trela and John Fenwick, who once ran Google’s satellite division, as well as former Aerospace Corporation executive Ashley Moore Williams.
The project is still in early stages and could be scrapped, but the goal is clear: a data link that bypasses cellular carriers. Apple may not need to build its own satellites; it could develop the ground‑side transmission hardware or the handset antenna that can lock onto existing low‑Earth‑orbit constellations.
If successful, the technology would let an iPhone send a text or a location ping even when no ground network exists. The concept mirrors the approach of Lynk, a startup that is building a LEO constellation to provide low‑bandwidth global roaming. Lynk’s service is limited to texting and calls, not high‑speed streaming.
Apple’s move could turn the iPhone into a truly always‑on device, but the engineering challenges are massive. Direct satellite links require precise antenna alignment and power management on a handset that is only a few centimeters across. Until Apple demonstrates a working prototype, the effort remains a high‑risk experiment that may never leave the lab.

## DARPA’s robotic servicing demo set for 2026
DARPA and its industry partners are gearing up to launch the Robotic Servicing of Geosynchronous Satellites (RSGS) program in summer 2026. The Mission Robotic Vehicle will carry a dexterous arm capable of on‑orbit upgrades, inspections, anomaly fixes, and even satellite relocation.
The effort is a joint venture between DARPA, the Naval Research Laboratory, NASA, and SpaceLogistics, a Northrop Grumman company. Program manager James Shoemaker emphasized that the demonstration aims to prove commercial viability for GEO servicing, opening the door for both government and private operators.
Geosynchronous orbit hosts hundreds of expensive communications satellites that are typically treated as disposable. A robotic service vehicle could extend their lifespans, reduce debris, and allow operators to add new payloads without launching a replacement. The technology also promises faster response to anomalies that would otherwise require a costly rescue mission.
The upcoming launch marks a shift from the “launch‑and‑forget” mindset that has dominated the sector for decades. If the MRV can successfully grapple a GEO satellite and perform a hardware swap, the economics of satellite fleets could change dramatically, making long‑term orbital assets more sustainable.

## What to watch
Watch for Anduril’s first operational contract, which will reveal whether its night‑vision sensor can survive a real combat environment. Track SpaceX’s next public statement on Starlink policy, especially any legal filings that could force the company to clarify its stance on military use. Apple is expected to file a patent on a handset‑compatible antenna within the next year; the filing will hint at how far the project has progressed. Finally, the RSGS launch in 2026 will be a litmus test for commercial on‑orbit servicing—success could trigger a wave of investment in GEO upgrade markets.
