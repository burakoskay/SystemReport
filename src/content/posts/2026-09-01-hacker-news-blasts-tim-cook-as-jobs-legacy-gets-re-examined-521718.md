## Tim Cook’s Reputation Under Fire on Hacker News
A Hacker News post titled "Tim Cook Sold Out Steve Jobs (2025)" rose to 132 points and attracted 165 comments within hours of posting. The headline alone framed Cook as betraying the vision of his predecessor, and the comment thread quickly split into two camps.

The post links to an article on anildash.com that argues Cook has prioritized short‑term shareholder returns over the product‑first ethos championed by Jobs.[^1][^2] Commenters cite the 2024 iPhone price hikes, the shift to services, and the decision to outsource key chip design work as evidence. Others counter that Cook has expanded the company’s market reach and introduced a profitable services portfolio. The debate is less about definitive facts and more about how much weight the community gives to legacy versus financial performance.

## AnkiDroid Loses Open Collective Donation Link on Google Play
Google Play recently enforced a policy that bars apps from including external donation URLs that point to Open Collective.[^3][^4][^5][^6][^7] AnkiDroid, the open‑source flashcard app for Android, was forced to remove its donation link from the store listing, as documented in issue #21656 on the project's GitHub repository.[^8][^3][^4][^5][^6]

The change means users can no longer click a “Donate” button in the Play Store to support the developers. The maintainers posted a brief update: the link was taken down after a review flagged it as a violation of the new “external payment” rule.[^3][^4] They are now exploring alternative channels, such as a dedicated website or in‑app purchase flow, but both options carry overhead and may alienate contributors who prefer the low‑friction Open Collective model.

## Open‑Source Funding Models Face Policy Headwinds
The AnkiDroid incident is part of a broader trend where platform policies intersect with the economics of free software. Open Collective, which lets projects collect donations without taking a cut, has become a popular conduit for community funding.[^5] However, recent Google Play guidelines treat any external link that solicits money as a potential violation, regardless of the platform’s transparency.[^3][^4]

Developers argue that the rule conflates legitimate fundraising with illicit monetization schemes. The policy effectively pushes projects toward Google’s own billing system, which imposes a 15‑30% fee and requires compliance with stricter user‑experience standards. Smaller projects, lacking the resources to integrate in‑app purchases, may see a drop in contributions, threatening sustainability. The community response has been vocal on forums and issue trackers, with many calling for an exemption for non‑commercial, donation‑only links.

## Community Responses and Technical Workarounds
Open‑source teams are not new to navigating constraints. A long‑standing Arduino hobbyist site lists dozens of DIY projects ranging from a car‑battery voltage logger to an e‑paper temperature monitor. The author openly shares schematics and code, inviting peer review and incremental improvement. When platform changes threaten distribution, the community often migrates to alternative hosting—GitHub, GitLab, or self‑served sites—preserving access to the underlying assets.

Similarly, Haskell enthusiasts publish "Aha!" moments on personal blogs to demystify functional programming concepts. Their posts avoid marketing fluff and focus on concrete trade‑offs: the steep learning curve versus the long‑term reduction in state‑related bugs. The same pragmatic tone appears in a recent Git facts series, where the author explains the mechanics of the staging area, stash references, and merge commit diffs without resorting to buzzwords. These examples illustrate a cultural resilience: when external pressures mount, developers double down on documentation, community support, and technical transparency.

## What to Watch
Watch for Google’s next policy clarification on external donation links; a revised guideline could restore Open Collective pathways or cement the shift toward in‑app billing. On the Apple side, monitor whether the criticism of Tim Cook’s strategic choices translates into boardroom pressure or shifts in product roadmap—especially as investors weigh legacy brand value against revenue growth. Both stories underscore a tension between platform governance and the open‑source ecosystem’s funding model; the next quarter will reveal whether communities adapt or retreat.
