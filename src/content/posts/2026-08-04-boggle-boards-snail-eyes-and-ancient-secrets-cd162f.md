## A Boggle Board That Beats All Odds

Dan Vanderkam finally proved the highest‑scoring Boggle layout. His 4×4 grid nets 3,625 points, a figure that eclipses the 2,195‑point board found in a 1982 study. The proof appears in a 2025 arXiv preprint (arXiv:2507.02117) and in Vanderkam’s own blog, where he describes a branch‑and‑bound search that grouped similar board patterns and pruned losers instead of brute‑forcing every combination.

The board contains more than 1,000 valid words; the longest is “replastering.” Vanderkam told the Financial Times he was the only person interested in the problem, a claim the 1982 effort disproves. Still, his method is a textbook example of how clever algorithmic pruning can solve combinatorial puzzles that were previously out of reach.

## Golden Apple Snails Offer a Glimpse of Human‑like Eye Regeneration

A Nature Communications paper this year shows golden apple snails can regrow their entire camera‑type eyes after injury. The eyes feature a cornea, a lens, and a retina packed with photoreceptors—structures remarkably similar to those in human eyes. Co‑author Alice Accorsi of UC Davis notes the snails thrive in laboratory settings, making them a practical model for studying the underlying genetic pathways.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5][^6][^7][^8]

If researchers can isolate the genes that trigger eye regeneration in these mollusks, the work could point toward therapies for human retinal damage. The study stops short of claiming a direct route to clinical treatments, but the biological parallel is striking enough to merit serious follow‑up.

## 3‑D Forensics Suggest the Shroud of Turin Is a Medieval Art Piece

Cícero Moraes built a digital 3‑D model of the Shroud of Turin and draped it over both a human figure and a bas‑relief carving. The Archaeometry paper (2025, DOI 10.1111/arcm.70030) concludes the bas‑relief scenario matches the shroud’s imprint far better, especially because the infamous “Agamemnon mask effect”—a widening of the face when fabric is flattened—does not appear.

Moraes’s work dovetails with a newly uncovered 14th‑century treatise by Nicole Oresme, cited in the Journal of Medieval History (2025, DOI 10.1080/03044181.2025.2546884). Oresme dismissed the shroud as a forgery, making his manuscript the earliest written skepticism on record, predating the 1389 account by Bishop Pierre d’Arcis of Troyes.

## Karnak’s Flood‑Formed Foundations Rewrite Egyptian Chronology

A geological survey published in Antiquity maps sediment cores and thousands of ceramic shards from Egypt’s Karnak Temple complex. The authors argue that around 2,520 BCE the Nile regularly flooded the site, creating an island of higher ground that attracted the first permanent settlement between 2,591 and 2,152 BCE.

As the river channels shifted over millennia, the usable land expanded, allowing the temple to grow outward. The new timeline aligns the temple’s earliest phase with the early Middle Kingdom, challenging older models that placed its foundation much later.

## What to Watch Next

Vanderkam will release his code repository next month, inviting others to test the branch‑and‑bound approach on related word‑game puzzles. Snail‑eye researchers plan a CRISPR knockout study to pinpoint the genes responsible for lens regeneration, a trial slated for early 2026. Meanwhile, a multidisciplinary team aims to publish a high‑resolution 3‑D scan of the Shroud’s fibers later this year, which could finally settle the debate over its manufacturing technique. Finally, Egyptologists expect a follow‑up sediment analysis from Karnak’s southern precincts, a dataset that could refine the flood chronology even further.

These disparate threads share a common thread: they turn curiosity‑driven questions into concrete data, forcing us to rewrite assumptions that have lingered for decades.
