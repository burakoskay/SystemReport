## Berlin Tenants Push for Expropriation Referendum
Tenants in Germany’s capital are collecting signatures to force a citywide referendum that would expropriate private housing firms owning more than 3,000 units. The proposal targets Deutsche Wohnen, Berlin’s largest private landlord, and other firms with comparable portfolios.

The campaign began at the end of February and was given four months to gather signatures from 7 % of eligible voters – roughly 175,000 people – to trigger a September vote. After two months the organizers announced on April 26 that 130,000 names had been submitted and that 75 % of the city‑verified signatures were deemed valid. If the threshold is met by the end of June, the referendum will appear on the ballot.

## Legal Backdrop: Court Ruling and Constitutional Limits
Last month Germany’s constitutional court overturned a five‑year rent‑freeze that had been in place since November of the previous year. The decision affected an estimated 1.5 million households in Berlin, opening the door to steep rent hikes and potential retroactive rent recovery.

Deutsche Wohnen benefitted directly from the ruling; its shares on the Frankfurt exchange rose nearly 3 % after the announcement. The company’s stock had been trending downward during the freeze, so the court’s reversal delivered a short‑term market boost.

## Historical Context: Berlin’s Housing Market Evolution
The city’s rental market has transformed dramatically since the 2008 financial crisis. A Rosa Luxemburg Foundation report found that half of Berlin’s housing stock is owned by a few thousand multimillionaires. Investors seeking safe havens after the crisis poured capital into Berlin, attracted by historically low rents.

By 2018 Berlin topped global rankings for rent growth, with average prices climbing more than 20 % over the previous year. Data from Guthmann Estate shows median rents rose over 70 % between 2012 and 2021. The surge has displaced long‑time residents, especially in neighborhoods such as Kreuzberg and Prenzlauer Berg, where gentrification accelerated after the fall of the Berlin Wall.

## Activist Strategies and Wider Controversies
The expropriation drive mirrors a broader pattern of activist groups targeting wealth and political influence. The Capytistas, a secretive collective that claims more than $1.2 million in backing, launched a campaign to dox Elon Musk and other billionaire‑linked figures, arguing that those who purchase political power deserve scrutiny.

Both movements share a tactical focus on exposing elite assets, but they differ in scope. Berlin’s tenants are seeking statutory change through a referendum, while the Capytistas employ direct exposure tactics that raise legal and ethical questions. The juxtaposition highlights a growing willingness among activist circles to confront entrenched economic power using unconventional methods.

## What to Watch
The signature deadline looms at the end of June. If organizers reach the 175,000‑signature threshold, Berlin voters will decide in September whether to transfer thousands of private apartments into public ownership. Legal challenges are likely, and the outcome could reshape housing policy not only in Berlin but across German cities grappling with rent inflation. Watch the city council’s verification process and any court filings that may arise as the referendum approaches.
