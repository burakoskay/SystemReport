Starcloud announced a probe destined for Alpha Centauri, turning a commercial data‑center venture into a deep‑space test.

The team that built orbital data centers now pitches a high‑risk mission that would leave the solar system entirely. No launch date or budget appears in the brief, but the ambition signals a shift from near‑Earth services to interstellar experimentation.

## The Probe That Aims Beyond the Solar System

Starcloud’s orbital facilities have hosted customers for years, but the company’s new concept pushes the envelope. The probe would travel more than four light‑years, a distance no private spacecraft has attempted. The plan rests on a “high‑risk” label, implying that success is far from guaranteed.

The announcement arrives without a technical roadmap, yet the intent is clear: commercial operators now see interstellar reach as a branding lever. If the probe reaches its target, the data‑center brand will be forever linked to a historic first.

## NASA’s Flight Opportunities: A Funding Bridge

NASA’s Space Technology Mission Directorate runs the Flight Opportunities program. The solicitation calls for proposals that can be tested on commercial sub‑orbital launch vehicles, reduced‑gravity aircraft, or high‑altitude balloons.

Proposals must be submitted by June 8 2018, with selections slated for November 2018. The program promises to move technologies from the lab to a relevant flight environment, reducing technical risk and validating feasibility.

The solicitation requires every bidder to register in the NSPIRES system and to identify an authorized organizational representative. NASA will purchase the flight directly from the chosen commercial provider, letting industry and academia pick the platform that best matches their hardware.

## Parallel Ambitions on Earth’s Atmosphere

Alteon, founded by a 20‑year‑old entrepreneur, is building autonomous aircraft that harvest wind energy to stay aloft for several months. The concept trades conventional fuel for continuous atmospheric power, aiming for endurance that far exceeds typical UAV missions.

If Alteon’s prototype can remain airborne for the promised duration, it would open new logistics pathways for remote sensing, communications relays, and persistent surveillance. The venture remains experimental, but the timeline mirrors the same “high‑risk, high‑reward” mindset seen in Starcloud’s interstellar probe.

## Policy Under a New Chair

Representative Lamar Smith is set to chair the House Committee on Science, Space, and Technology next year. Smith’s résumé includes a stint as chair of the Judiciary Committee and authorship of the controversial SOPA bill.

Smith’s ascent places a longtime legislator at the helm of the committee that oversees NASA, the NSF, and the EPA. Critics argue that his record on internet policy foreshadows a cautious, possibly status‑quo‑preserving approach to emerging tech. Supporters point to his experience navigating complex legislative terrain.

The committee’s agenda will shape funding streams for programs like Flight Opportunities and influence regulatory pathways for private space ventures. Smith’s leadership could therefore affect how quickly projects such as Starcloud’s probe or Alteon’s wind‑harvesting aircraft move from concept to flight.

## What to Watch

Watch the November 2018 announcement from NASA’s Flight Opportunities office; it will reveal which commercial and academic teams receive flight slots. Track Starcloud’s timeline for a launch contract, as any partnership with a launch provider will signal progress. Monitor Alteon’s test flights for proof of multi‑month endurance, which could attract defense or telecom customers. Finally, follow the hearings and budget hearings that Rep. Smith’s committee will hold once he assumes the chair, because those sessions will set the tone for federal support of high‑risk space and atmospheric technology.
