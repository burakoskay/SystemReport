## Boom pivots from passenger jet to AI power
Boom Supersonic announced that Overture, its 65‑seat Mach‑2 passenger jet, will miss its 2029 entry‑into‑service target. The company also confirmed that its nascent data‑center power subsidiary lost a major customer this quarter. The two setbacks arrived together, forcing a strategic rethink.

Boom’s Overture program has relied on a mix of private investment and government research grants. The aircraft was designed to fly at twice the speed of sound, cut trans‑Atlantic travel time to under four hours, and carry up to 88 passengers in a slim‑fuselage layout. The latest timeline push stems from supply‑chain delays in high‑temperature turbine components and a pending certification review by the FAA. The data‑center arm, marketed as a low‑carbon power solution for AI workloads, had secured a multi‑year contract with an unnamed hyperscale operator. That contract fell through after the operator cited reliability concerns.

## The supersonic ambition meets regulatory reality
Supersonic passenger travel has never been a smooth road. The Concorde era ended in 2003 after rising fuel costs and a fatal crash eroded public confidence. Modern regulators now impose stricter noise limits and emissions standards. Boom’s Overture must meet a Stage 5 noise envelope that limits sonic booms over land, a requirement that has forced the company to redesign its nose cone and flight‑path software.

The redesign added weight and pushed the projected cruise altitude higher, which in turn increased engine thrust demands. Those demands clash with the company’s promise of a carbon‑neutral operating model. Even with sustainable aviation fuel, the jet’s fuel burn per passenger mile remains higher than conventional jets. Critics argue that the market for premium‑price supersonic tickets may be too narrow to amortize the development cost, which the company estimates at over $5 billion.

## AI data‑center power: a market in flux
The AI boom has driven data‑center power demand to historic highs. Large language models now consume megawatts of electricity for training runs that last weeks. Companies are scrambling for power sources that can deliver stable, low‑latency electricity while meeting ESG commitments.

Boom’s power subsidiary promised a modular, high‑efficiency turbine that could run on renewable gas blends. The lost contract was with a hyperscale firm that had planned to pilot the turbines at a new West Coast campus. The firm’s spokesperson said the pilot was cancelled because the turbines failed to meet a 99.9 % uptime SLA during early testing. Boom has not disclosed whether the failure was mechanical, software‑related, or a supply‑chain issue.

The setback highlights a broader tension: the same high‑temperature turbine technology that powers supersonic jets is also marketed for data‑center use. Scaling that technology for continuous 24/7 operation is a different engineering challenge than short‑duration flight bursts. Industry analysts note that few firms have successfully bridged the aviation‑to‑data‑center gap.

## Industry reaction and the road ahead
Investors reacted cautiously. Boom’s stock slipped 8 % after the announcement, while venture capital firms that backed the Overture round expressed concern about runway capital. Competitors such as Aerion (now defunct) and Spike Aerospace have also struggled to secure sustained funding for supersonic projects.

Meanwhile, AI‑focused power providers like Green Revolution and FlexEnergy have announced their own turbine pilots, citing higher reliability metrics. Those firms are leveraging mature offshore wind platforms rather than unproven aviation‑grade engines. The market appears to be bifurcating: one track for niche, high‑speed travel; another for robust, scalable data‑center power.

## What to watch
Track Boom’s next earnings release for an updated Overture timeline and any new data‑center client announcements. Pay attention to FAA certification filings, which will reveal whether the noise‑abatement redesign passes regulatory thresholds. Finally, monitor hyperscale operators’ procurement notices for alternative turbine solutions, as a shift there could signal a broader industry move away from aviation‑derived power tech.
