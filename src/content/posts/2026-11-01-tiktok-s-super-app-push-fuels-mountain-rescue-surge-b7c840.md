Mountain‑rescue teams in England and Wales were called out every day of 2024, a pattern driven by a flood of 18‑ to 24‑year‑olds chasing TikTok‑fueled photo spots.

Rescues jumped 24% between 2019 and 2024, according to Mountain Rescue England and Wales data analysed by Ordnance Survey. The 18‑24 age group saw its calls double from 166 to 314, while Scotland recorded a historic 1,000 callouts for the first time. The surge aligns with a TikTok‑led push to become the app people use for most digital activities, a move the company is reportedly testing across payments, restaurant guides and more.

## A record‑breaking year for rescue volunteers

Volunteers reported that the daily callouts strained their ability to juggle full‑time jobs and family commitments. Mike Park, chief executive of Mountain Rescue England and Wales, said the nonstop demand “is part of the story” and that teams often handle multiple incidents simultaneously. The Lake District, Snowdonia and the Peak District topped the list of busiest locations, with south Wales, Northumberland and Cornwall following closely.

Older adults still needed help. The 40‑44 cohort logged 196 callouts, and rescues of people aged 75‑79 doubled from 56 to 112. Even the over‑80 bracket rose to 93 rescues, up from 75 in 2019. The data underscores that while young people dominate the headlines, risk spans generations.

## TikTok’s ambition to be a super app

A recent TechCrunch report notes that TikTok is experimenting with features that could turn the platform into a one‑stop shop for digital life. The company is testing integrated restaurant guides, payment flows and other services that mirror the functionality of established super apps in Asia. If successful, TikTok would sit at the center of both social interaction and everyday transactions.

The super‑app vision matters because it deepens the platform’s grip on user attention. When a single app curates entertainment, commerce and communication, users spend more time within its ecosystem. That extra screen time translates into more exposure to location‑based trends, such as the “honeypot” spots that flood TikTok feeds with dramatic cliff‑side selfies.

## Social feeds meet shaky navigation tools

Mountain‑rescue officials blame “honeypot” locations for the surge. Instagram and TikTok posts showcase remote peaks, hidden waterfalls and rugged trails, prompting followers to replicate the shots. The problem compounds when users rely on generic navigation apps that lack offline maps or detailed terrain data. Park observed that “more and more people are tempted into risky locations by Instagram posts and the navigation apps being used aren’t always suitable for an outdoor environment.”

The mismatch between glossy visuals and inadequate navigation creates a perfect storm. A hiker may follow a viral route, only to discover a missing trail marker or an unexpected cliff. Without paper maps or reliable offline GPS, a misstep can turn a day hike into a rescue operation. The Guardian’s analysis of Ordnance Survey data shows Good Friday as the busiest outdoor day in 2024, a day when many first‑time visitors head into national parks.

## Strain on volunteer rescue infrastructure

Volunteer rescue teams operate on limited resources. The daily callout pattern forces teams to split attention across parallel incidents, stretching equipment and personnel thin. Park warned that the “unpaid volunteers, their employers, friends and families” bear the hidden cost of the surge. In the Lake District, rescue coordinators expressed concern that the current demand is unsustainable without additional funding or professional staff.

The financial model of volunteer rescues relies on donations, charitable grants and occasional government support. A sudden spike in callouts threatens to outpace those income streams. Moreover, the emotional toll on volunteers—who often confront life‑threatening situations—can lead to burnout, further eroding the capacity to respond.

## What to watch next

The next quarter will reveal whether TikTok’s super‑app experiments include built‑in safety features, such as verified trail data or offline map downloads. Regulators may also pressure navigation‑app developers to meet higher accuracy standards for outdoor use. For mountain‑rescue services, tracking the volume of “honeypot”‑driven calls will indicate whether education campaigns or app‑level interventions can blunt the trend. Keep an eye on policy proposals from the UK Department for Digital, Culture, Media & Sport and on any partnership announcements between TikTok and official outdoor safety bodies.
