## Razr Flex leaks challenge iPhone Duo

Motorola's Razr Flex appears in full‑color renders, positioning itself as a direct rival to Apple's iPhone Duo. The device sports a wide, foldable form factor that flips open to a tablet‑sized screen, a layout the iPhone Duo also advertises.

9to5Google published the images on Tuesday, showing the phone in two colorways and confirming the dimensions that give it a noticeably larger unfolded display than the original Razr flip. The leak also reveals a hinge design that appears sturdier than the earlier Razr 5G, suggesting Motorola has refined the mechanical engineering that plagued its first generation of foldables.

The foldable market has been a slow burn since Samsung's Galaxy Z series proved the concept viable. Motorola's last attempt, the Razr 5G, stumbled on durability concerns and a premium price tag that limited adoption. By targeting the iPhone Duo niche—consumers who want a single device that doubles as a small tablet—Motorola hopes to capture a segment that Apple barely serves. If the Flex can deliver a reliable hinge and a competitive price, it could force Apple to rethink its dual‑screen strategy or double down on the iPhone Duo’s higher‑end positioning.

## Google Fi adds floating toolbar and Ask Fi

Google Fi's Android client now includes a floating toolbar that hovers above the app’s main view, letting users switch between plans, view data usage, and access support without digging through menus. The change is part of a modest navigation redesign that Google rolled out last week.

According to 9to5Google, the new toolbar also introduces an "Ask Fi" button that launches a contextual chat assistant. Users can type or speak queries like "How many megabytes do I have left?" and receive instant answers drawn from their account data. The floating element stays visible as users scroll, reducing the number of taps required to manage carrier settings.

Floating toolbars have become a common pattern in first‑party Android apps, offering a compromise between full‑screen menus and hidden drawers. By adopting the UI, Google Fi aligns itself with the broader Android ecosystem while giving power users a quicker path to the information they need. The tweak is unlikely to win awards, but it smooths a friction point that long‑time Fi customers have complained about.

## Microsoft ends To Do on Mac and iOS

Microsoft announced the retirement of its To Do task‑management service, pulling the Mac and iOS applications from their respective stores. Existing users will lose access to the apps after a grace period, and Microsoft advises them to migrate tasks to Outlook or Microsoft 365's Planner.

9to5Mac detailed the rollout, noting that the Windows version of To Do will continue to operate, but the cross‑platform sync feature will cease once the mobile clients are disabled. The company did not provide a public timeline for the shutdown, leaving enterprise teams to scramble for alternatives.

The move fits into a larger shift in Microsoft's productivity roadmap. Over the past year the firm has consolidated many of its personal‑productivity tools under the Outlook brand, positioning the email client as the hub for tasks, notes, and calendar events. By retiring To Do on Mac and iOS, Microsoft reduces duplication of effort and nudges users toward a single, cloud‑backed experience. The decision hurts power users who preferred To Do’s lightweight interface, but it also streamlines the company’s focus on a unified suite.

## Europe tightens tech oversight: TikTok, Apple encryption, Matrix rollout

Germany and Austria have jointly called for an EU‑wide mechanism to control TikTok, arguing that the short‑form video platform poses risks to data privacy and political manipulation. The two governments submitted a formal request to the European Commission, urging the bloc to draft legislation that would give regulators the power to audit the app’s algorithmic recommendations and enforce data‑localisation rules.

The request surfaces amid growing scrutiny of TikTok’s handling of European user data. While the platform has pledged greater transparency, critics say voluntary measures fall short of the stringent standards applied to larger tech firms. If the EU adopts the proposed controls, TikTok could face mandatory data‑center construction within the bloc or be forced to share its recommendation engine with independent auditors.

Across the Channel, the UK government was forced to acknowledge a secret court order that compelled Apple to create a backdoor for encrypted iMessage traffic. The revelation, reported by Hacker News, shows that British authorities can legally require a tech giant to weaken its own security architecture under national‑security pretexts. Apple has not commented on the specifics, but the episode fuels the ongoing debate about encryption, privacy, and state surveillance.

Meanwhile, the European Commission completed a massive rollout of the Matrix open‑source communication platform to more than 40,000 staff members, according to a blog post highlighted on Hacker News. The migration replaces legacy chat tools with an end‑to‑end encrypted service that the EU says will improve internal collaboration while meeting strict data‑protection mandates.

These three developments illustrate a broader trend: European governments are moving from passive regulation to active enforcement of digital infrastructure. Whether it is demanding algorithmic transparency from TikTok, compelling a backdoor from Apple, or standardising secure messaging across the Commission, the continent is signaling that tech firms can no longer operate under the assumption of regulatory inertia.

## What to watch

Motorola is expected to confirm the Razr Flex specifications at a launch event later this year; the timing of that announcement will reveal whether the company can beat Apple to market with a functional dual‑screen competitor. Microsoft’s To Do shutdown will prompt enterprise IT teams to audit their task‑management pipelines and possibly accelerate migration to Outlook or third‑party solutions. In Brussels, the European Parliament’s upcoming committee hearing on TikTok will test the political will to impose EU‑wide controls, while the UK courts are set to hear arguments on the legality of the Apple encryption order. Finally, the European Commission’s Matrix deployment will be monitored for adoption rates and any security incidents that could shape future policy on open‑source communications. Tracking these milestones will show how quickly regulators can translate intent into enforceable tech policy.
