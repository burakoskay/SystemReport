## Energy deal reshapes Google’s carbon strategy
Google announced it will buy half the electricity from a Finnish nuclear power plant. The move secures a stable, low‑carbon supply for its data centers. BBC reports the agreement covers a single reactor, but the contract does not disclose the plant’s name or capacity.
The deal signals a shift in how large cloud providers source power. Instead of relying on renewable intermittency, Google is locking in baseload generation. Critics argue nuclear remains politically sensitive, especially in Europe. Supporters point to the low‑emissions profile compared with coal or gas.
If the contract holds, Google will offset a sizable portion of its global energy demand. The company has pledged to run on carbon‑free energy 24/7 by 2030. This purchase could accelerate that timeline, but it also ties Google to the regulatory environment of Finnish nuclear policy.

## Mexican teen turns sound into fire suppression
A 16‑year‑old student in Mexico built an acoustic fire extinguisher that uses sound waves to put out flames in seconds. Upsocl details that the device generates a focused ultrasonic pulse that displaces air and starves the fire of oxygen.
The prototype was demonstrated on a small gasoline fire. The student reports the extinguishing time was under three seconds. No chemicals were involved, and the system resets instantly. The report does not provide quantitative data on energy consumption or scalability.
If the concept scales, it could complement existing suppression methods. However, the technology faces challenges in delivering sufficient acoustic energy for larger fires. Safety certifications and field testing remain pending.

## System76 pushes AI workstation memory limits
System76 unveiled the Thelio Mira AI workstation, a Linux box that ships with 192 GB of GPU memory. The product page lists a single NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPU as the source of the massive buffer.
The machine targets developers who train large language models or run high‑resolution simulations. By exposing the full memory pool, System76 hopes to reduce the need for multi‑GPU stitching. The workstation runs Pop!_OS, a Debian‑based distro tuned for AI workloads.
Pricing and availability are not disclosed in the announcement. Early reviewers note the thermal design is aggressive, and the chassis is larger than the company's standard models. The launch underscores a growing market for on‑premise AI hardware, even as cloud providers dominate compute.

## NTSB updates on Miami Boeing 767 runway excursion
The NTSB released an investigative update on the Boeing 767 runway excursion that occurred in Miami. The press release states the aircraft overran the runway during landing, prompting an evacuation of passengers.
Preliminary findings point to a combination of wet runway conditions and possible brake system anomalies. The board has not released a final cause determination, and the report urges airlines to review runway friction assessments.
The update arrives weeks after the incident, keeping the accident in the public eye. Aviation analysts say the case may influence future runway safety standards, especially at airports with similar surface drainage issues.

## Revisiting Cherenkov radiation as a physics reminder
The IAEA explains Cherenkov radiation as light emitted when charged particles travel faster than light does in a medium. The effect produces a characteristic blue glow, famously seen in nuclear reactors.
The phenomenon occurs because the particle outruns the electromagnetic wavefront, forcing the medium to emit photons. The IAEA article notes the effect is used in particle detectors to infer particle speed.
While the principle is well‑established, the recent Hacker News discussion highlights lingering public fascination with “faster‑than‑light” concepts. The physics remains a concrete illustration of limits imposed by media rather than vacuum speed of light.
