## The Startup That Turned a Burger Joint Into a Perk Test

David Segura walked into a Santa Monica burger joint with a simple ask: a corporate discount for his six‑person video startup. The manager shrugged. The team’s weekly beef‑patties with manchego and aioli were a habit, but the numbers didn’t add up for a traditional bulk‑order deal. Segura left with a badge on Foursquare that called him the restaurant’s “Mayor,” but no price break.

That dead‑end conversation sparked a search for a tool that could give tiny teams the same perk leverage that Fortune 500s enjoy. The answer landed in a new platform launched earlier this year by Los Angeles‑based BetterWorks.

## BetterWorks' Playbook for Small Business Perks

BetterWorks lets a company subscribe for a fee that scales with headcount—$30 to $500 a month—and then allocate a monetary allowance to each employee. The employee logs into a marketplace, picks a vendor within a two‑mile radius, and redeems a discount that can range from 10 % to 70 %.

Founder and CEO Paige Craig explains the premise: “Small‑business owners can't afford to bring in a six‑figure consultant to build a perks program, and they don't have the time to design one on their own.” The platform handles billing, delivery, and the negotiation of discounts, freeing managers from the back‑and‑forth that usually stalls perk roll‑outs.

To date the service supports roughly 17,000 employees across 184 companies, most of them in Los Angeles and San Francisco. Vendors that sign up for free contribute a slice of each transaction, and the marketplace now lists close to 2,400 local businesses—from coffee shops to massage studios. BetterWorks reports that it is adding hundreds of new vendors each week.

## The Competitive Landscape: From Groupon to AI Game Builders

At first glance BetterWorks resembles a modern Groupon clone: a middleman that aggregates discounts and takes a cut. Groupon’s early growth proved the model’s scalability, but vendors quickly learned that one‑off deal seekers rarely become repeat customers. The thin margins left many merchants wary of deep‑discount programs.

BetterWorks tries to sidestep that pitfall by decentralizing the purchase decision. Employees choose where to spend their allowance, and the platform’s algorithm surfaces vendors that already enjoy foot traffic in the employee’s neighborhood. The result is a higher likelihood of repeat business, because the discount is tied to a location the employee already frequents.

The same “platform‑first” mindset is emerging elsewhere. Matiks, a new AI‑driven game‑creation service, raised $10 million to let users build puzzles and math games without writing code. While Matiks targets a different market—indie developers rather than corporate HR—it shares BetterWorks’ belief that a low‑friction, self‑serve interface can unlock value for users who lack deep technical resources.

## Platform Play: How Tesla, Turo, and BetterWorks Signal a Shift

Tesla’s recent partnership with car‑sharing service Turo adds another data point. Eligible Turo hosts receive a one‑month free trial of Tesla’s Full Self‑Driving (FSD) software, effectively lowering the barrier for small fleet operators to test autonomous features. The move mirrors BetterWorks’ strategy: give a powerful tool to a segment that would otherwise face prohibitive upfront costs.

Both deals illustrate a broader trend—large technology firms courting the “mid‑market” of small businesses and independent operators. Instead of selling a monolithic suite, they package a specific capability (perks management, AI game creation, autonomous driving) as a plug‑and‑play service. The economics shift from large, upfront license fees to recurring, usage‑based pricing.

For BetterWorks, the advantage is twofold. First, the subscription model aligns revenue with the number of active users, encouraging the company to grow its employee base rather than chase a handful of massive contracts. Second, the marketplace approach creates network effects: more vendors attract more employees, and more employees attract more vendors.

## What to Watch

BetterWorks aims to hit 25,000 employees by the end of next year while expanding beyond the West Coast. The next data point to track is the churn rate of both employees and vendors—if the platform can keep participants engaged, it may prove that a small‑biz perks model can scale without the discount fatigue that plagued early daily‑deal sites. Simultaneously, watch how Tesla’s FSD trial with Turo hosts impacts adoption rates among independent drivers; a surge could prompt other OEMs to launch similar low‑entry programs. Finally, Matiks’ $10 million raise positions it to iterate quickly—its progress will signal whether AI‑driven creator tools can break out of the niche hobby market and become a staple for small developers.

## The Road Ahead for Small‑Biz Platforms

If BetterWorks can sustain its vendor growth while maintaining healthy margins, it may force larger HR SaaS providers to rethink how they bundle perks. Companies like Gusto and Rippling already offer benefits administration, but none provide a fully self‑service marketplace with real‑time discount negotiation. A successful BetterWorks model could push incumbents toward open‑API integrations or even acquisitions.

The underlying question isn’t whether small businesses want perks—it's whether they can access them without handing over a chunk of their payroll to a consulting firm. BetterWorks claims to have cracked that puzzle with a few minutes of setup and a transparent pricing tier. The next few quarters will reveal whether the market buys the solution or sticks with the status‑quo of ad‑hoc vendor negotiations.
