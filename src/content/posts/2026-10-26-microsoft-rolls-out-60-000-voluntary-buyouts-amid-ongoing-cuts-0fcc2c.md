Microsoft is offering voluntary buyouts to roughly 60,000 employees, a move that could affect more than a quarter of its global headcount. The offer arrives on the heels of two years of layoffs that have already trimmed the workforce by about 30,000 people.

Engadget reports the buyout pool covers 60,000 staff, while TechCrunch frames the program as the latest cost‑cutting lever in a broader restructuring effort. Both outlets note that the figure represents a sizable slice of Microsoft’s total employee base, underscoring how deep the company is digging to reshape its balance sheet.

## The buyout wave

The voluntary buyout package is presented as a financial incentive for employees to exit on their own terms. Microsoft has not disclosed the exact payout structure, but the sheer scale—60,000 names—signals a willingness to pay for headcount reduction rather than rely solely on involuntary layoffs. By offering a choice, the firm hopes to avoid the morale damage that forced cuts can cause.

For workers, the decision hinges on personal calculus: severance versus the security of staying in a company that has historically prized stability. The program’s timing suggests the company is trying to pre‑empt further layoffs while still meeting aggressive cost targets set by the board.

## A decade of layoffs erodes the old pact

Microsoft’s reputation for job security dates back to the early 2000s, when an informal “pact” promised lower‑than‑market pay in exchange for long‑term stability. That bargain began to fray after the 2008 downturn and accelerated under Satya Nadella’s tenure, when the firm eliminated forced stack‑ranking and opened the door to more aggressive workforce adjustments.

Layoffs.fyi records indicate that Microsoft shed about 30,000 employees in the last two years, roughly 13 % of its total staff. Those cuts already broke the long‑standing expectation that Microsoft would rarely, if ever, lay off staff. The new buyout offer feels like a continuation of that shift, moving the company further away from its historic employment model.

## Engineers feel the squeeze

The latest round of reductions hit software engineers hard. Bloomberg cited a 3 % global staff cut, translating to almost 7,000 jobs. In Washington state alone, more than 40 % of the roughly 2,000 positions eliminated were engineering roles, according to local reporting.

High‑profile developers have spoken out. Mike Droettboom, a principal engineering manager and CPython core contributor, posted that the cancellation of Microsoft’s support for the Faster CPython project coincided with his team’s layoffs. Ron Buckton, a veteran TypeScript engineer with a decade on the project, announced his departure after 18 years. Azure SDK veteran Matt Podwysock and Gabriela de Queiroz, director of AI for Microsoft for Startups, also confirmed they were let go.

These departures arrive amid Nadella’s claim that AI now writes about 30 % of Microsoft’s code. While the company denies a direct causal link between AI adoption and the layoffs, the coincidence fuels speculation that automation is reshaping staffing decisions. At the same time, Microsoft’s $80 billion investment in AI‑enabled datacenters may be tightening the budget for traditional engineering salaries.

## Cost strategy meets AI ambition

Microsoft’s cost‑cutting measures appear to be a two‑pronged approach: reduce headcount through buyouts and layoffs, then redirect savings toward AI infrastructure. The $80 billion earmarked for AI‑centric datacenters suggests the firm sees cloud‑based AI services as a growth engine that can offset the expense of a leaner workforce.

Critics argue that the trade‑off risks losing deep engineering talent just as the company leans on AI to differentiate its cloud offering. The loss of seasoned developers—especially those with niche expertise in Python, TypeScript, and Azure SDKs—could slow the delivery of new features that AI‑driven services depend on.

Supporters counter that a smaller, more flexible talent pool can move faster, especially when paired with AI tools that automate routine coding tasks. If the AI claim holds, the company may be able to maintain output while paying fewer salaries, but the transition will test how well machine‑assisted development can replace human experience.

## What to watch

The next quarter will reveal whether Microsoft’s buyout program curtails the need for additional layoffs. Stakeholders should monitor the company’s quarterly earnings for clues about headcount‑related expense reductions and any updates on AI‑driven code contributions. The rollout of the $80 billion AI datacenter plan and the performance of Azure’s AI services will also indicate whether the cost‑saving gamble pays off or leaves the firm scrambling for talent.
