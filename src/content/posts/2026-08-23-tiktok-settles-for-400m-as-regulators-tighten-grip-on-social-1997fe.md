TikTok agreed to pay $400 million to settle a DOJ claim it breached the Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act. The settlement ends a two‑year investigation that began after the Justice Department alleged the video app collected data from minors without parental consent.

The deal, reported by TechCrunch, caps the government’s monetary exposure but does not admit wrongdoing. TikTok will also implement a suite of technical safeguards designed to verify user age and limit data sharing. The company must submit compliance reports to the Federal Trade Commission for the next three years. The settlement arrives as Congress debates stricter COPPA enforcement and as other platforms confront parallel child‑safety lawsuits.

## Meta’s looming court showdown

Meta is back in federal court over child safety, according to WIRED. The lawsuit targets core features of Facebook and Instagram, arguing that the companies fail to protect minors from harmful content. Plaintiffs seek injunctive relief that could force Meta to redesign news feeds, comment sections, and advertising algorithms.

The case is described as “landmark” because it could reshape how social networks surface user‑generated material. If the court grants the requested changes, Meta may have to embed age‑gating mechanisms at the platform level. The company has signaled willingness to negotiate, but its legal team has not disclosed a settlement figure. The outcome will likely influence how other social media firms address COPPA‑related risks.

## Influencer box hype fuels a shadow market

A separate WIRED report details how content creators are buying empty luxury boxes to stage unboxing videos. Influencers claim the practice adds “authenticity” to their feeds, while resellers profit from the scarcity of the sealed packaging. Prices for the boxes have surged, with some resellers listing them for “crazy” multiples of the original retail cost.

The trend blurs the line between genuine product promotion and manufactured hype. Brands receive exposure without shipping actual goods, but consumers may be misled about product availability. Regulators have not yet issued formal guidance, but the practice raises questions about deceptive marketing under existing consumer‑protection statutes.

## Hidden Zillow listings distort NYC rent market

Ars Technica reports a lawsuit alleging that Zillow created hidden listings that artificially reduced perceived housing supply in New York City. The concealed inventory, the suit claims, made it harder for renters to find affordable units and contributed to higher rent prices.

The complaint argues that Zillow’s algorithmic filters suppressed certain listings from public view, effectively manufacturing a supply shock. Plaintiffs seek damages and an injunction requiring Zillow to make all listings visible. The case highlights how platform design choices can have real‑world economic impacts, especially in tightly constrained housing markets.

## What to watch next

The TikTok settlement sets a precedent for how the DOJ may pursue COPPA violations against other apps. Watch for FTC compliance reports that could reveal whether the technical safeguards are sufficient. Meta’s case will next be argued before a federal judge; a ruling that mandates feature redesign would ripple through the social‑media ecosystem. Meanwhile, consumer‑protection agencies may scrutinize influencer box schemes and hidden‑listing algorithms for deceptive practices. Tracking regulatory filings and any settlement announcements will indicate how quickly the industry adapts to heightened legal pressure.
