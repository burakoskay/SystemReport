Google announced seven years of OS and security updates for the Pixel 8 and Pixel 8 Pro. The pledge means the phones will receive the latest Android builds and monthly security patches through 2030.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5][^6][^7][^8]

The blog post that introduced the commitment specifies “Feature Drops every few months” in addition to the core OS upgrades.[^1][^3][^6] Previously Google limited Pixel support to three years of OS upgrades and five years of security fixes.[^3][^4][^7] Samsung, meanwhile, just rolled out a four‑year security update program that applies to devices released as far back as 2019, covering more than 130 models.

## Google’s seven‑year pledge for Pixel 8

The Pixel 8 series is the first Google flagship to carry a formal seven‑year support timeline.[^3] Under the plan, owners will see Android 20 at least by 2029 and possibly Android 21 by 2030, assuming Google keeps its annual release cadence.

Google ties the extended support to its in‑house Tensor processor. Controlling the silicon lets the company push driver updates and security fixes without waiting on third‑party chipset vendors. That advantage is why competitors that rely on Qualcomm or MediaTek often stumble when a chipset reaches end‑of‑life.

The move also puts Google ahead of most Android rivals. Samsung, OnePlus and Xiaomi currently promise four generations of Android version upgrades and five years of security patches for their newest flagships. Apple’s iPhone X, released in 2017, received five major iOS updates before support tapered off, so Google’s seven‑year window is a notable escalation.[^4]

## Samsung’s four‑year security promise and its limits

Samsung’s latest update policy guarantees three years of major OS upgrades plus four years of security patches for a swath of Galaxy devices. The company emphasizes “monthly or quarterly” security updates depending on device age, with newer phones getting monthly patches and older models shifting to a quarterly cadence.

The rollout covers flagship, mid‑range, and tablet lines dating back to the Galaxy S9 (2018) and even the Galaxy S8, which now receives quarterly security fixes. Samsung cites collaboration with OS and chipset partners and more than 200 carriers worldwide to meet the schedule.

Despite the longer security window, Samsung still lags in delivering OS upgrades. The firm took three months to start the Android 11 rollout, whereas Google, OnePlus, Oppo and Xiaomi launched their updates faster. Samsung also opts out of Google’s Android beta program, a channel that speeds up early‑stage testing for many OEMs.

## Why the update gap persists across Android OEMs

The fragmented update landscape stems from hardware diversity and the reliance on external chipset makers. When Qualcomm or MediaTek stop supporting a processor, OEMs must either abandon updates or invest heavily in custom drivers. Fairphone’s recent five‑year OS promise and eight‑year security plan illustrate how a company can control the entire stack, but its limited market reach keeps it from reshaping the broader ecosystem.

Google’s own Android source code receives monthly security patches covering the four newest Android versions, dating back to Android 8.1. In theory, Google could extend Pixel support to match Samsung’s four‑year security window plus three years of OS upgrades, but the company has chosen a longer, more public commitment instead.

Consumer expectations also play a role. Many users keep phones for six or more years, especially in markets where device replacement costs are high. Without a clear, long‑term update promise, buyers risk ending up with devices that cannot receive the latest privacy or performance improvements.

## Tools and workarounds: community solutions like Obtainium

For users stuck on older hardware, third‑party tools can fill the update void. Obtainium, an Android app that pulls releases directly from GitHub, GitLab, F‑Droid and other sources, lets power users install and update apps without relying on the Play Store’s rollout schedule.

The app supports custom filters to handle diverse release schemes and even offers a generic HTML scraper for obscure sources. While Obtainium does not replace OS updates, it demonstrates that the community can mitigate the lag in official patch delivery, especially for open‑source utilities that receive frequent upstream releases.

## What to watch

Google’s seven‑year pledge will be tested when the first major Android version beyond 20 lands in 2029. Keep an eye on whether Google maintains its annual release cadence and whether it extends the security window beyond 2030. Samsung’s next update calendar will reveal if the firm can shorten its OS rollout lag while keeping the four‑year security promise. Finally, watch how other OEMs—especially those using custom silicon like Xiaomi’s Surge or OnePlus’s Snapdragon‑based flagships—respond to the new benchmark set by Google and Samsung. The next wave of announcements will indicate whether the Android update race is truly accelerating or merely shifting the finish line.
