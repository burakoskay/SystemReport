The Senate confirmed a Trump FCC nominee while the administration abandoned two other high‑profile science appointments, and Apple filed a courtroom objection to a government‑mandated iPhone backdoor. The three moves expose how partisan appointments and privacy battles are reshaping the tech policy arena.

The Cruz‑led Senate Commerce Committee voted 15‑0 to approve the FCC nominee, with all Democrats walking out to deny a quorum.[^1][^2][^3][^4] The same week, the White House pulled its FDA nominee and the Senate scrapped a vote on the NSF pick after Senator Bill Cassidy warned that "this time requires leadership and fortitude."[^5][^6][^7][^8] Meanwhile, Apple argued in federal court that the All Writs Act cannot be stretched to force the company to create a new operating system that would act as a backdoor for the FBI’s San Bernardino iPhone 5C.

## Cruz Committee Pushes Through FCC Nominee

Senator Ted Cruz chaired the Commerce Committee that approved the FCC nominee with a unanimous 15‑0 tally.[^1][^2][^3][^4] Democrats staged a walk‑out, hoping to prevent a quorum and to protest what they called a lie from nominee Thumann about his lack of media‑issue work.[^1][^2][^3][^4] The vote proceeded despite the boycott, delivering the first confirmed Trump‑appointed FCC commissioner in months.[^1][^2][^3]

The confirmation clears the path for the FCC to move forward on its agenda, but the partisan split signals that future regulatory battles—especially over broadband and net neutrality—will be fought in a highly politicized arena. With the committee’s vote in hand, the full Senate is expected to ratify the nominee next week, barring any procedural setbacks.[^1][^2][^3]

## Trump Withdraws FDA Nominee and NSF Vote Dies

In a separate development, the White House withdrew its FDA nominee, citing an inability to secure Senate support.[^5][^6][^7][^8] The Senate also abandoned a vote on the National Science Foundation pick after a brief debate.[^5][^7] Senator Bill Cassidy, a Republican from Louisiana, summed up the mood: "This time requires leadership and fortitude."[^5]

The dual retreat highlights the administration’s waning leverage in confirming science‑policy officials. With the FDA and NSF leadership gaps widening, industry groups warn that delayed appointments could stall critical research funding and drug‑approval timelines, especially as the nation grapples with post‑pandemic health challenges.

## Apple Fights Backdoor Demand in San Bernardino Case

Apple filed a motion on Tuesday arguing that the government’s request for a custom iPhone decryption tool oversteps the All Writs Act. The act, a procedural statute, historically compels third parties to assist law enforcement, but Apple contends it was never meant to force a company to write new software that weakens its security.

The motion cites the 2015 San Bernardino incident, where the FBI sought access to an iPhone 5C used by shooter Syed Rizwan Farook. Apple maintains that creating a "backdoor" would expose hundreds of millions of users to potential hacks. The company’s brief quotes the government as trying to "rewrite history" and portrays the All Writs Act as a "magic wand" rather than a limited tool. Tech giants including Facebook, Google, and Microsoft, along with civil‑rights groups, have filed amicus briefs supporting Apple’s stance.

## AI’s Uncertain Impact on Government Jobs

A think tank commissioned by former Prime Minister Tony Blair asked ChatGPT to forecast AI’s effect on public‑sector employment. The model’s answers ranged from optimistic to "wonky," reflecting the broader uncertainty that surrounds AI‑driven automation.

OpenAI’s 2023 paper estimated that 80 % of workers have at least 10 % of their tasks exposed to large language models, while 19 % face more than half of their tasks at risk. The report listed occupations like mathematicians and lawyers among those fully exposed. Critics argue that focusing on whole jobs inflates the "affected" count and understates actual job loss, because most roles retain core human elements.

The debate mirrors past technology shifts: the steam engine expanded demand for pack animals, while the internal combustion engine eventually made working horses obsolete. AI may carve out a similar niche—automating repetitive analysis while preserving human judgment in nuanced decision‑making. Policymakers will need to decide whether to accelerate retraining programs or let market forces dictate the transition speed.

## What to Watch

Watch the full Senate vote on the FCC nominee next week; a close floor vote could signal how much bipartisan friction remains. Track the Biden administration’s next move on the FDA and NSF vacancies—whether new nominees will be proposed or the seats stay empty. Follow the court’s ruling on Apple’s motion; a decision that expands the All Writs Act could set a precedent for future tech‑law enforcement clashes. Finally, keep an eye on forthcoming AI impact studies from government agencies, as they will shape funding priorities for workforce reskilling in the public sector.
