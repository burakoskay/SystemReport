## OpenAI pushes ChatGPT memory to free users

OpenAI announced that the memory feature in ChatGPT will roll out to its free tier. The feature lets the model retain user preferences across sessions, reducing the need to repeat context. OpenAI says the upgrade makes interactions feel more natural and cuts friction for casual users.

The company described the change as a "smarter" memory system that can learn about you over time. No pricing details were released, but the move signals a shift toward broader personalization without a subscription barrier. Critics note that persistent memory raises privacy questions, especially when the data lives on OpenAI’s servers. The firm has not disclosed how long stored preferences remain or what deletion controls exist.

Engineers will likely probe the implementation for data leakage risks. The feature’s debut on the free tier could pressure competitors to match the convenience, potentially accelerating a privacy‑vs‑personalization arms race in the chatbot market.

## Samsung flips back to Snapdragon for the Galaxy Z Flip 8

A leak reported by 9to5Google indicates that Samsung’s upcoming Galaxy Z Flip 8 will ship with Snapdragon chips alongside its Exynos 2600. The report suggests the Snapdragon option will address performance complaints tied to the Exynos line, even though the chip carries a higher bill of materials.

The dual‑chip strategy mirrors Samsung’s earlier approach with the Galaxy S 23 series, where Snapdragon powered U.S. models and Exynos served other regions. Analysts see the move as a concession to consumer demand for consistent performance across markets. The flip phone’s design remains unchanged, and the device will continue to compete with the new Trailseeker SUV‑inspired edition.

If Samsung follows through, the Snapdragon variant could boost benchmark scores by 10‑15 percent, according to historical data. However, the cost premium may narrow profit margins on a device already priced at a premium for its foldable form factor.

## Cloudflare expands AI edge services with VoidZero acquisition

Cloudflare announced that VoidZero, a startup focused on AI‑accelerated edge computing, is joining its platform. The blog post on Cloudflare’s site details the integration, emphasizing faster inference and lower latency for AI workloads.

VoidZero’s technology specializes in compressing large language models for edge deployment. By folding that capability into Cloudflare’s global network, the company aims to reduce the distance between users and AI inference nodes. The acquisition could make Cloudflare a more attractive partner for developers seeking to serve AI‑driven features without relying on centralized data centers.

Industry observers caution that the move may intensify competition with rivals like Fastly and Akamai, which are also courting AI edge use cases. Cloudflare has not disclosed the purchase price, but the deal underscores the growing value placed on on‑premise AI acceleration as a differentiator in the CDN market.

## Google employees meme the company’s own AI

Internal memes circulating among Google staff portray the firm’s AI products as underperforming. A collection of screenshots shared on Hacker News shows jokes about the AI’s “suckiness,” highlighting a gap between internal expectations and external hype.

The memes suggest that engineers are frustrated with the quality of Google’s generative models, despite the company’s public narrative of leadership in AI. No official comment has been issued, and the posts do not reference a specific product, but the sentiment aligns with broader industry chatter about the difficulty of delivering reliable conversational agents at scale.

Such internal criticism may pressure product teams to prioritize robustness over flashy releases. If the memes reflect genuine technical setbacks, Google could see delays in upcoming AI features that are slated for integration across its suite of services.

## A $65,000‑a‑year Manhattan homeschooling center raises questions

Alpha School opened a Manhattan campus that charges $65,000 per student annually. Internal documents obtained by WIRED reveal the company’s launch mantra: “Opening date > safety.” The phrase suggests that meeting a rollout deadline took precedence over thorough safety checks.

The center positions itself as a premium alternative to traditional schools, but it is technically a homeschooling hub rather than an accredited institution. Critics argue that the model blurs regulatory lines and may exploit affluent families seeking elite education experiences.

The high price tag places the campus out of reach for most families, reinforcing concerns about widening educational inequality. As other private operators consider similar tuition structures, policymakers may need to examine how homeschooling centers are classified and regulated in dense urban markets.
