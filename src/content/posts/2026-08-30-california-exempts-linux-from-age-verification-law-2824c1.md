## California exempts Linux from age‑verification mandate

The California legislature passed a unanimous amendment that removes software distributed under the GPL, MIT, BSD and Apache licenses from the state's age‑verification law. The change was reported by Tom's Hardware, which noted that the exemption applies to any Linux‑based operating system and related open‑source tools.

Lawmakers framed the exemption as a protection for developers who cannot feasibly embed age‑checks into low‑level system components. The bill does not alter the broader requirement that adult‑content websites verify users' ages, but it carves out a narrow technical category that would otherwise be forced to implement costly compliance measures.

The amendment also signals a rare bipartisan consensus on a technology‑policy issue. All 80 members of the Assembly and Senate voted in favor, according to the legislative record. No dissenting votes were recorded, and the measure cleared both chambers without amendment.

## Why the exemption matters for developers and users

Open‑source developers often ship binaries that run on a wide range of hardware, from personal laptops to embedded devices. Requiring those binaries to perform age verification would add runtime overhead and potentially break compatibility with legacy systems. By exempting the licenses, California avoids imposing a technical burden that could slow updates and increase security risk.

For end users, the exemption preserves the seamless experience of installing and running Linux distributions. Users will no longer encounter prompts asking for age confirmation when installing core system packages, a scenario that would have been confusing and unnecessary.

The change also sets a precedent for how states can tailor digital regulations without stifling the open‑source ecosystem. Regulators in other jurisdictions may look to California's approach when drafting similar laws, especially as more states consider age‑verification mandates for online content.

## Corporate contributions shape the open‑source ecosystem

A recent analysis by Aiven, an open‑source cloud data platform, quantified corporate activity on GitHub during the fourth quarter of 2022. The study counted contributors, repositories, and commit volume across public projects.

Microsoft led the commit count with 128,247 changes, edging out Red Hat's 125,012 and Google's 94,961. In terms of distinct contributors, Google topped the list with 5,757 developers, followed by Microsoft at 5,513 and Red Hat at 3,656. Intel contributed 2,834 developers and 36,948 commits, placing it fourth in both metrics.

Amazon Web Services (AWS) climbed to the fifth spot, overtaking IBM, according to Aiven CTO Heikki Nousiainen. "An unexpected result of our research was seeing Amazon overtake IBM to become the fifth biggest contributor," Nousiainen said. The data underscores that the largest open‑source contributors are corporate entities, not hobbyists.

These numbers matter because corporate involvement drives the maintenance of critical infrastructure projects that underpin cloud services, networking, and security. When companies allocate engineering resources to open‑source code, they also gain influence over project direction and standards.

## Training pipeline fuels open‑source talent

The Linux Foundation announced that a cumulative one million learners have completed its Linux and open‑source software courses. The milestone was reached through a partnership with edX, the nonprofit MOOC platform operated by Harvard University and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology.

Clyde Seepersad, general manager of Linux Foundation training, said the figure reflects a growing demand for open‑source expertise. "While we're humbled at having reached one million folks, we remain aware of just how big the need is," he said. Seepersad highlighted that the Foundation's blockchain course attracted over 65,000 registrations within its first 100 days.

The foundation's curriculum includes free entry‑level introductions to Linux, OpenStack, and other hot topics. It also offers two certification tracks—Linux Foundation Certified System Administrator (LFCS) and Linux Foundation Certified Engineer (LFCE)—which employers cite as proof of practical skill.

The surge in training aligns with the hiring data from Dice's Open Source Jobs Report, which indicates that 60 % of surveyed companies are actively seeking full‑time open‑source talent. As corporations deepen their code contributions, the pipeline of trained professionals becomes a strategic asset.

## What to watch

California's exemption will be codified in the upcoming implementation guidelines for the age‑verification law, expected to be published by the Department of Consumer Affairs within the next 30 days. Stakeholders should monitor whether the exemption is referenced in the final rulebook and whether any litigation arises from industry groups that oppose the carve‑out.

On the corporate front, Aiven plans to release a quarterly follow‑up report on open‑source contributions. Tracking changes in commit volume and contributor counts will reveal whether the current leadership of Microsoft and Google persists or if other players, such as AWS, continue to gain ground.

Finally, the Linux Foundation will publish enrollment numbers for its 2024‑2025 training cycle in early Q2. Observing growth trends in certification uptake will indicate whether the talent pipeline keeps pace with the escalating demand for open‑source engineers.

Stakeholders across policy, corporate, and education sectors should keep these data points on their radar to gauge the evolving balance between regulation, corporate stewardship, and workforce development in the open‑source landscape.
