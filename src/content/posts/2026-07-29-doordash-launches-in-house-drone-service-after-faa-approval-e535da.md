## FAA green light turns DoorDash Air from prototype to commercial service
DoorDash has cleared the FAA’s Part 135 hurdle and is ready to launch DoorDash Air, its own drone delivery service. The certification lets the company operate a commercial drone fleet across the United States.
The Federal Aviation Administration named DoorDash one of eight operators with Part 135 air carrier status, joining Wing, Amazon’s Prime Air and Flytrex. DoorDash built the Air drones in‑house through its DoorDash Labs robotics and autonomy team, the same group that created the Dot delivery robot. "We want drone delivery to work for any merchant, anywhere," said Harrison Shih, head of DoorDash Air, in a company statement. The drones and supporting ground infrastructure were designed in the U.S., and most components are domestically sourced.

## Shifting the middle‑mile: why drones matter for DoorDash logistics
DoorDash reports that more than 20 percent of its 2023 orders fell in the three‑to‑five‑mile range, but those trips took roughly 25 percent longer than shorter deliveries. The delay stems from the difficulty of finding drivers willing to travel farther for a single order.
By assigning those mid‑range orders to drones, DoorDash hopes to keep human couriers on short hops that maximize earnings per minute. Its Autonomous Delivery Platform will automatically pick the optimal mode—human driver, DoorDash Air drone, or the ground‑based Dot robot—for each order. The company says the shift will let drivers stay near dense merchant clusters, improve routing efficiency, and increase take‑home pay.

## San Francisco warehouse becomes the first dedicated drone testbed
Last month DoorDash leased a 34,325‑square‑foot warehouse at 1960 Folsom St. in the Mission District, roughly two miles from its headquarters. The lease anticipates about 200 employees working on research, development and testing of autonomous delivery technologies.
The letter to San Francisco’s zoning administrator spells out the test parameters: outdoor flights will be limited to roughly 150 feet altitude, no more than two drones in the air at once, and each flight capped at 30 minutes. Most testing will occur inside the facility, but the company will also conduct limited outdoor runs during regular business hours in a gated parking area.

## The broader drone delivery battlefield
DoorDash joins a crowded field that already includes Wing, Amazon Prime Air, Flytrex and several smaller pilots. The company has been running limited‑scale drone deliveries in Texas, Virginia, North Carolina and Australia, often in partnership with Wing or Flytrex. In June, DoorDash and Flytrex launched a service in Dallas‑Fort Worth that can carry up to 6.6 pounds and delivers from restaurants such as Papa Johns and The Brass Tap.
While DoorDash touts its in‑house stack, it also says it will continue collaborating with Wing and Flytrex on certain markets. That dual approach mirrors the strategy of other incumbents, which blend proprietary hardware with third‑party networks to achieve scale without over‑investing in every component.

## What to watch: rollout timeline and regulatory ripples
DoorDash says it aims to begin commercial deliveries with DoorDash Air drones this fall, using the San Francisco warehouse as a launchpad. Observers will track the first live orders, the reliability of the autonomous handoff system, and any follow‑up guidance from the FAA on flight density limits. A successful fall rollout could push DoorDash into direct competition with Amazon and Wing on the high‑value middle‑mile segment, while also testing whether the promised driver earnings boost materializes.
