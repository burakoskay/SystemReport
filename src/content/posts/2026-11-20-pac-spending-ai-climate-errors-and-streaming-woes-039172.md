## A PAC splurged on steak while candidates got pennies

The latest filing shows a political action committee poured more money into a steak dinner and a luxury club than into any campaign it supports. MAHA PAC’s expense report lists a high‑end steak dinner and membership fees at an exclusive club that together outstrip the total cash it gave to candidates.

An expert called the spending pattern "pretty unusual" for a PAC, which normally directs the bulk of its budget to ads and direct contributions. The filing, reported by Ars Technica, details the exact line items: a steak dinner for several donors and a monthly luxury‑club fee that together exceed the sum of all candidate donations recorded for the same period.

## LLMs stumble on climate facts, even the best models

A new analysis of large language models reveals a systematic failure to provide accurate climate information when asked directly. The study, also covered by Ars Technica, tested several commercial and open‑source models with climate‑related queries.

Some models performed slightly better than others, but every model misreported at least one key metric, such as average global temperature rise or carbon‑budget estimates. The report notes that the errors share a common pattern: the models hallucinate numbers that look plausible but diverge from the scientific consensus. The authors caution that developers cannot rely on LLMs to supply trustworthy climate data without additional verification.

## Mouse fight club hints at why we crave violent media

Researchers built a "mouse fight club" to see whether observing fights could substitute for actual combat experience. The experiment, described in Ars Technica, let mice watch other mice spar before being placed in a real arena.

The observers improved their performance, but only halfway to the level of mice that fought directly. The authors conclude that visual exposure to aggression provides some learning, yet it does not fully replace the sensorimotor feedback of live combat. The finding offers a biological clue for why humans enjoy watching violent sports or movies: the brain picks up partial cues without the full cost of participation.

## Netflix’s app can hide the shows you actually want

Netflix CEO Ted Sarandos admitted that the platform’s interface sometimes buries desirable content beneath unrelated titles. In an interview with Ars Technica, Sarandos explained that algorithmic recommendations and the endless scroll can push a hit series down the list, making it hard for users to discover it without searching.

He described the problem as a gap between the company’s intent to surface popular shows and the reality of a cluttered UI. The admission signals that even the biggest streaming services struggle to balance personalization with discoverability, a tension that can frustrate power users who expect precise control over their watchlists.

## What to watch next

Watch for the Federal Election Commission’s response to the MAHA PAC filing; any enforcement action could set a precedent for how PACs justify non‑candidate expenses. Track updates from AI research labs as they introduce verification layers to curb climate‑data hallucinations. Follow Netflix’s upcoming UI redesign roadmap, which the company promises will address the “buried content” issue in the next quarterly release. Each of these developments will show whether the tech ecosystem can correct the missteps highlighted above.
