## Steam's Achievement Sets beta aims to lock 100% runs
Steam launched Achievement Sets in beta on Monday. The feature groups all achievements for a title into a single set that ignores post‑launch DLC. Players who finally hit a perfect run no longer see their record erased when a new expansion adds extra trophies.
The change matters for speedrunners and completionists who track 100% completion as a badge of honor. By separating base‑game milestones from future add‑ons, Steam protects the effort that can take dozens of hours.
Critics note that the beta does not retroactively apply to games already flagged with DLC‑dependent achievements. Early adopters will have to wait for a full rollout before legacy titles receive the same protection.

## Congress eyes game ownership rights
Representative Ritchie Torres announced plans to introduce a bill that would compel the Federal Trade Commission to set rules guaranteeing that consumers can play the games they buy. The proposal follows a wave of complaints that digital storefronts can revoke access or restrict usage.
Torres’ draft cites the need for clear licensing language and a baseline right to run purchased software on any compatible device. The bill would give the FTC authority to enforce those standards across platforms.
Industry groups have responded with mixed signals. Some argue the measure could stifle innovation, while others welcome a legal safety net for gamers who fear losing access to titles they own.

## The intern who kept Steam alive
A 2002 lawsuit between Valve and Sierra Entertainment over Counter‑Strike cyber‑cafe licensing nearly bankrupted the fledgling studio. Vivendi, Sierra’s parent, flooded Valve with Korean‑language documents that slowed the case.
Valve hired a summer intern named Andrew, a Korean‑language major at UCLA. He sifted through thousands of pages and uncovered a single document showing Vivendi had destroyed evidence. That find turned the tide in Valve’s favor.
Winning the case secured the Half‑Life and Counter‑Strike franchises for Valve, reimbursed legal costs, and forced Vivendi to cease cyber‑cafe distribution. With the rights in hand, Valve built its own distribution channel, which later evolved into Steam.
Steam now powers the same Achievement Sets feature that aims to preserve 100% runs. The intern’s work, though obscure, underpins the platform that millions rely on for game delivery and community features.

## GG Translator tries to clean up toxic chat
A new open‑source app called GG Translator lets players speak blunt in‑game taunts and have them rephrased into polite requests. The tool uses the OpenAI API to rewrite phrases like “Holy shit can someone fucking help this noob sniper!” into “Can someone please lend a hand to our friend Sniper?”
GG Translator works on any game with voice or text chat, including Dota 2, Overwatch, League of Legends, Fortnite, and Counter‑Strike. Users press a hotkey, speak, and the app returns a cleaned‑up version that can be sent to Discord, TeamSpeak, or the game’s chat window.
The program is free to download but requires an OpenAI API key, which incurs low usage fees. It runs on Windows and Mac, needs a microphone and a virtual audio cable, and stores all data locally.
While the app does not claim to be 100 % accurate, early testers say it reduces friction in multiplayer matches without violating Steam’s terms of service.

## What to watch
Steam will expand Achievement Sets beyond the beta in the coming months, and Valve may reveal pricing or API details for developers. Keep an eye on the FTC’s response to Torres’ bill; a ruling could reshape digital‑rights contracts across the industry. Finally, watch community adoption of GG Translator as toxicity‑reduction tools gain traction in competitive scenes. The next patch, regulation, or update could shift how gamers earn achievements, retain ownership, and communicate on‑line.
