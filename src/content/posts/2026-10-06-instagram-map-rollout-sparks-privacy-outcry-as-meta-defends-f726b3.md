## Instagram pushes map feature and read‑only Plus mode
Instagram launched a map tool on Wednesday that shows users' last active locations. The same day Meta announced a read‑only mode for Instagram Plus subscribers, which Engadget dubbed a nicer name than “stalker mode.”[^1][^2]

Both moves landed on a platform already under legal fire for features that critics say fuel teen addiction. The map rollout adds a fresh privacy vector to a debate that has been simmering for years.[^3][^4][^5]

## How the map is supposed to work
Meta markets the Instagram map as a way to “stay up‑to‑date with friends.” Users can opt in to share their last active location with a custom list of friends. The feature lives at the top of the Direct Messages inbox and is rolling out to the US today, with global availability promised later.[^6][^7][^8]

According to Meta’s blog, location sharing is off by default and requires “double consent by design.” The app first asks if you want to share, then asks you to confirm the audience. Only people you follow back or a private list you select can see your location.[^6][^7][^8]

## Users report unexpected exposure
Despite the double‑consent claim, several Threads users said they saw their location on the map without opting in. One user wrote, “Mine was set to on and shared with everyone in the app. My location settings on my phone for IG were set to never.” Another complained that the update arrived “without any warning.”

The backlash mirrors earlier concerns about Snapchat’s Snap Map, which also let friends see real‑time locations. Critics argue that any default‑off system still creates a frictionless path to accidental sharing, especially for users who are not tech‑savvy.[^8]

## Meta’s public response
Instagram chief Adam Mosseri addressed the complaints on Threads, noting that “people are confused and assume that, because they can see themselves on the map when they open, other people can see them too.” He said the company is “double checking everything” and reiterated that location sharing requires explicit consent.

A Meta spokesperson echoed Mosseri, stating that “Instagram Map is off by default, and your live location is never shared unless you choose to turn it on.” The spokesperson added that only followers you approve can view shared locations.[^8]

## The feature sits amid broader legal scrutiny
The map launch arrives as a bipartisan coalition of attorneys general from 42 states files lawsuits accusing Meta of deceptive practices that harm teens. The suits allege that Instagram’s algorithmic feed uses “variable reward schedules” that act like a slot machine, driving compulsive scrolling.[^4][^5]

The complaint cites internal research showing that Instagram’s design “more damaging to mental health than cyberbullying.” It also points to the default‑visible Like count as a comparison engine that fuels anxiety. Meta counters that it has rolled out over 30 tools, including parental controls and usage limiters, to protect young users.[^3][^5]

About 22 million U.S. teenagers use Instagram daily, according to the lawsuit. The states seek an injunction to stop Meta from deploying allegedly deceptive features and request unspecified fines. A related case is already before a judge in Oakland, California.[^3][^5]

## Technical trade‑offs and open questions
The map feature blurs the line between optional social discovery and covert surveillance. While the double‑consent flow adds a barrier, the UI still displays a personal pin as soon as the map opens, which can be misread as a public signal.

Engineers must decide whether to hide the user’s own pin until they actively share. That would add privacy but reduce the map’s “lightweight way to connect” narrative. The current design also raises questions about how location data is stored and who can query it for ad targeting.

## What to watch
The next weeks will reveal whether Meta tightens the map’s consent flow or rolls back the feature in response to the backlash. Watch for a statement from the Federal Trade Commission, which has signaled interest in the ongoing state lawsuits. A court ruling that forces Meta to modify its algorithmic feed could also reshape how location data feeds into content recommendations.

If Meta adjusts the map or introduces clearer warnings, the change will set a precedent for how large platforms balance social discovery with privacy safeguards. If the lawsuits succeed, we may see a broader clampdown on features that encourage endless scrolling.
