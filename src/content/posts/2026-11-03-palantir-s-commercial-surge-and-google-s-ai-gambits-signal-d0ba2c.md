Palantir's latest quarter tipped its U.S. commercial revenue above government sales, a milestone that flips a long‑standing revenue model. At the same time Google is reshaping two very different fronts: it is demanding schools abandon its search platform to use AI tutoring tools, and it has dismantled a botnet that infected over a million Android TV boxes.

TechCrunch reported that Palantir's U.S. commercial revenue surpassed U.S. government revenue for the first time ever in the most recent quarter. Ars Technica detailed Google's new policy that lets schools opt into AI tools that "purportedly don't give away the answers" but forces them to give up Google Search. The same outlet also disclosed that Google cut ties with the company behind a botnet campaign that hijacked 1.3 million Android TV boxes.

## Palantir's commercial breakthrough

The revenue crossover marks the first instance Palantir has earned more from private‑sector contracts than from its historic government clientele. For years the firm built its reputation on defense and intelligence contracts, then leveraged that credibility to sell data platforms to corporations. The shift suggests that commercial customers are now willing to pay premium prices for the same analytics capabilities that once lived behind classified firewalls.

Analysts see the move as a validation of Palantir's strategy to diversify its addressable market. The company's quarterly filings show a steady climb in commercial bookings, but the exact dollar amount remains undisclosed. What matters is the signal to investors: Palantir can sustain growth without relying on the cyclical nature of government spending. If the trend holds, the firm may double down on industry‑specific solutions for finance, health care, and manufacturing.

Critics caution that commercial expansion brings new competitive pressures. Cloud giants and pure‑play analytics firms already dominate many of the sectors Palantir now targets. The company must prove its platform can integrate with existing data stacks faster than rivals. Failure to do so could stall the momentum generated by this quarter's headline.

## Google forces schools to trade search for AI tutoring

Google announced a new education offering that pairs AI‑driven tutoring tools with a requirement that participating schools disable Google Search for students. The company frames the move as a way to prevent AI from simply handing out answers, but the trade‑off is stark: schools lose access to the world's most used search engine.

The policy hinges on a set of proprietary tools that generate hints rather than full solutions. Ars Technica noted that the tools "purportedly don't give away the answers," a phrase that signals uncertainty about the efficacy of the approach. Schools must sign a contract that cedes control of their search ecosystem, effectively handing Google a larger slice of the education market.

Educators have responded with a mix of curiosity and concern. Some see value in guided problem‑solving, while others fear the loss of a critical research tool. The decision also raises antitrust questions, as Google leverages its dominance in search to push a nascent AI product. If the rollout expands, the company could set a precedent for bundling AI services with core infrastructure.

## Google dismantles a massive Android TV botnet

In a separate security move, Google announced it has severed ties with the entity responsible for a botnet that compromised 1.3 million Android TV devices. The botnet turned smart TVs into a distributed denial‑of‑service (DDoS) platform, siphoning bandwidth and exposing users to privacy risks.

Google's response involved cutting off the malicious actor's access to its advertising and cloud services, effectively starving the operation of revenue streams. The company also pushed firmware updates to patch the vulnerability that the botnet exploited. Ars Technica reported that the takedown was conducted without public fanfare, reflecting Google's preference for quiet, technical fixes.

The incident underscores the growing attack surface of IoT devices. While smartphones receive frequent security patches, many smart TVs languish with outdated software. By targeting the botnet's financial lifeline, Google demonstrated a willingness to intervene directly in the supply chain of malicious actors. The move may encourage other platform owners to adopt similar tactics.

## What to watch next

Investors will monitor Palantir's next earnings release for evidence that commercial growth can outpace the inevitable slowdown in government contracts. A sustained lead in private‑sector revenue could reshape the company's valuation model.

Educators and regulators should keep an eye on how Google's school AI program evolves. The requirement to abandon Search could trigger legal challenges or prompt competing platforms to offer alternative bundles. Meanwhile, the botnet takedown sets a precedent for tech firms to police the ecosystems that power their devices. Future disclosures about similar IoT threats will reveal whether Google's approach becomes a new industry standard.
